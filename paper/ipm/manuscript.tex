\title{Repeated-walk bias in debiased graph propagation for recommendation: an exact correction and its limits}

% Anonymised for double-anonymised review: author details are given on the
% separate title page (title_page.tex).

\begin{abstract}
Graph recommenders propagate preferences along walks of a logged graph whose
edges exist only where an item was exposed. Debiasing re-weights edges by
inverse propensities or completes them with doubly robust (DR) estimates. For
multi-hop propagation this is biased: a walk that traverses an edge several
times multiplies its estimate with itself. We give the exact bias and remove it
with DRUP (Doubly Robust, walk-Unbiased Propagation), which replaces each
repeated edge by its idempotent counterpart; Möbius inversion extends the
correction to any odd number of hops. The guarantees require nuisances that do not depend on the log.
In simulation, cross-fitting suffices only if the degree weights are fixed a priori;
with degrees taken from the log, as in our experiments, the correction no longer reduces bias. The correction
changes no ranking: on Coat, Yahoo!\,R3, KuaiRand and KuaiRec its accuracy equals
that of uncorrected DR propagation, and the most accurate method differs by
dataset. It matters for score values, but only under oracle conditions: on a
semi-synthetic KuaiRec log it removes an 18--29\% overestimate of an
allocation's utility when propensities are true and weights fixed; with
estimated propensities all operators overstate it by 100--540\%. The
sample split that satisfies the assumptions costs up to 0.06 nDCG on sparse logs.
A real-data thinning audit of exposure invariance has limited power: its negative
control fails where invariance holds, and the selected configurations are not
invariant on Yahoo!\,R3 and KuaiRand. Private release is useful only on the largest log.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================

Graph-based recommender systems represent users and items as nodes and
logged interactions as edges, and they score a user--item pair by aggregating
signals along multi-hop walks~\citep{he2020lightgcn,shen2021gfcf}. The logged
graph, however, is not the preference graph. An edge exists only if the
platform \emph{exposed} the item and the user then responded, so the graph
records the exposure policy as much as the user's taste. A recent survey of
causal learning in graph-based recommendation~\citep{yu2026survey} traces
several open problems of the field, among them fairness, interpretability and
out-of-distribution behaviour, to message passing over this observational
graph, and it lists theoretical guarantees and privacy as open directions.

Two families of corrections exist. \emph{Loss-level} debiasing re-weights the
training objective by inverse propensities or doubly robust (DR)
terms~\citep{schnabel2016,wang2019drjl,saito2020unbiased}, but it leaves the
propagation operator of a graph model untouched. \emph{Propagation-level}
debiasing changes the propagated graph itself, for example by weighting each
edge by its inverse propensity~\citep{kim2022navip} or by learning
popularity-aware aggregation weights~\citep{zhou2023apda,dpaa2026}. This paper
starts from a simple observation about the second family. An inverse-propensity
edge estimate $W_e=O_eY_e/p_e$ is unbiased for the potential outcome $Y_e$,
but a walk that traverses $e$ twice contributes $W_e^2$, and
$\E[W_e^2]=Y_e/p_e\neq Y_e$. The same happens for DR edge estimates. Walks that
repeat an edge are part of every multi-hop operator: the back-and-forth step
$u\to j\to u$ of a three-hop walk is one. Their excess is largest where the
propensity is smallest, that is, on the rarely exposed edges that debiasing is
meant to recover.

We make this observation precise and remove the resulting bias exactly. Four
points qualify its practical reach, and we state them up front. First, the
guarantees need nuisances (propensities, imputation, degree weights and score
constants) that do not depend on the log. Cross-fitting the propensity and the
imputation suffices if the degree weights are fixed a priori, but not if they are
computed from the log, which our experiments do; in that case the correction no
longer reduces bias in simulation. A sample split does supply independent
nuisances, and we treat this variant, DRUP-split, as the estimator that the theory
covers. Second, for ranking the \emph{unexposed} candidates of a user with
inverse-propensity edges, the repeated walks through the candidate vanish at three
hops, and the bias only appears from five hops on; for DR edges it is present at
three hops. Third, on four public benchmarks removing the bias does not change
top-$K$ accuracy or rankings, also under exposure caps, and the most accurate
method differs by dataset. Fourth, the correction matters where score values are
used, but only under oracle conditions: on a semi-synthetic log built from
KuaiRec's fully observed matrix, uncorrected propagation overestimates the
full-exposure utility of an allocation by 18--29\%, and the corrected operator is
within 2\%, provided that the propensities are correct and the propagation weights
are fixed a priori; with estimated propensities all operators overstate utility by
100--540\%. The contribution is therefore a diagnosis and an exact correction at the
level of scores, not an accuracy gain, and we report where the evidence for each
further property is weak: the thinning audit of exposure invariance, the private
release and the certificates (Section~\ref{sec:disc}).

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Diagnosis.} We give the exact conditional bias of inverse-propensity
and DR graph propagation on unexposed candidates. It is unbounded as
propensities vanish, and under a propensity clip $\tau$ its worst case is
$\Theta(1/\tau)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}).
\item \emph{Estimator.} DRUP (Doubly Robust, walk-Unbiased Propagation) uses
$Y_e^2=Y_e$ for binary outcomes and replaces every repeated edge by its
idempotent unbiased counterpart. The three-hop operator has the asymptotic
cost of the uncorrected one (one item Gram matrix plus $O(mn)$ reductions) and
factorises into a public item operator and per-user corrections. Möbius
inversion over the coincidence patterns of walk indices makes the correction
exact for any odd number of hops (Theorem~\ref{thm:khop}). A sample split
supplies nuisances that do not depend on the edge estimates.
\item \emph{Guarantees.} With nuisances that do not depend on the log, DRUP is
edge-wise doubly robust for propagation over the full-exposure graph
(Theorem~\ref{thm:unbiased}); on unexposed candidates it is unbiased up to the
imputation error at the candidate itself (Corollary~\ref{cor:cand}); its bias
under exposure dependence within a user's history is of second order in the
imputation error (Proposition~\ref{prop:dep}); we bound its variance and
clipping bias (Theorem~\ref{thm:var}); and its expected scores do not respond
to interventions on an item's exposure (Theorem~\ref{thm:fair}). Properties
that follow from linearity alone (attributions, certificates, joint
differential privacy, capped allocation) hold for any linear operator and are
in the Supplementary Material.
\item \emph{Evaluation.} We check the guarantees and their scope by Monte
Carlo, including the gap between the theory and cross-fitted nuisances; test
score values and score-based decisions on a semi-synthetic KuaiRec log with
known propensities; and compare DRUP with up to 26 tuned baselines on Coat~\citep{schnabel2016},
Yahoo!\,R3~\citep{marlin2009collaborative}, KuaiRand~\citep{gao2022kuairand} and
KuaiRec~\citep{gao2022kuairec}, whose test data are random or fully observed,
with per-split validation for every method, an analysis of tuning budgets,
and user-level tests with Holm correction. Real-data thinning interventions,
with a negative control, test exposure invariance.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
%======================================================================

We review the lines of work that DRUP touches. Table~\ref{tab:related}
summarises how DRUP relates to representative methods.

\paragraph{Graph-based recommendation}
Graph neural recommenders learn user and item representations by propagating
embeddings over the user--item graph. NGCF~\citep{wang2019ngcf} stacks
non-linear propagation layers, and LightGCN~\citep{he2020lightgcn} shows that
removing feature transformations and non-linearities improves accuracy, which
reduces propagation to a weighted sum of powers of a normalised adjacency.
UltraGCN~\citep{mao2021ultragcn} approximates infinite-layer propagation with a
constraint loss, and SGL~\citep{wu2021sgl} and SimGCL~\citep{yu2022simgcl} add
contrastive views. Closed-form models are strong competitors of trained
propagation: EASE~\citep{steck2019ease} learns an item--item matrix with a
zero diagonal in closed form, and EDLAE~\citep{steck2020edlae} generalises the
constraint by penalising the diagonal, which, like our diagonal correction of
$G$, removes an item's own contribution to its score; GF-CF~\citep{shen2021gfcf} and
BSPM~\citep{choi2023bspm} interpret collaborative filtering as graph signal
filtering, and graph convolutions can trade accuracy for
diversity~\citep{isufi2021accuracy}. Reproducibility studies show that carefully
tuned simple baselines often match graph models~\citep{anelli2023myth}. All these
models propagate over the \emph{logged} graph. DRUP keeps the linear
propagation form of LightGCN and of closed-form filters but propagates over an
unbiased estimate of the full-exposure graph, and we compare it with EASE and
GF-CF on both graphs.

\paragraph{Bias and debiasing in recommendation}
Recommendation logs are missing not at random: users see what the system
exposed and rate what they chose to rate~\citep{marlin2009collaborative}.
Surveys organise the resulting biases and their remedies~\citep{chen2023bias}
and cast debiasing as causal inference~\citep{gao2024causalsurvey}.
Propensity-based methods re-weight the loss by inverse exposure
probabilities~\citep{schnabel2016}, possibly self-normalised to reduce
variance~\citep{swaminathan2015snips}; \citet{saito2020unbiased} extend them
to implicit feedback, and \citet{liang2016exposure} model exposure as a
latent variable. Doubly robust learning combines inverse propensities with
an imputation model~\citep{wang2019drjl}, and later variants reduce its bias
and variance~\citep{guo2021mrdr,dai2022gdr,li2023stabledr} or learn
information-theoretic representations~\citep{wang2020cvib}. A small uniformly
logged sample can supervise the debiasing itself~\citep{chen2021autodebias}.
Causal approaches to popularity bias remove its direct effect by
counterfactual inference~\citep{wei2021macr}, adjust for it as a
confounder~\citep{zhang2021pda}, or disentangle interest from
conformity~\citep{zheng2021dice}. In this journal, popularity debiasing has
been studied from the user and item side jointly~\citep{boratto2021connecting},
with a controllable accuracy trade-off~\citep{okamura2024flexibly}, through
invariant learning with biased imputation~\citep{bai2025invariant}, and by
causal deconfounding of multi-criteria ratings~\citep{guo2026confounding}.
These methods correct the loss or the output of a model. DRUP applies IPS and
DR estimation to the \emph{edges} of the propagated graph, and its
control-variate family interpolates between the two.

\paragraph{Debiasing inside graph propagation}
Closest to our work are methods that change neighbour aggregation.
NAVIP~\citep{kim2022navip} weights each edge by its inverse propensity inside
LightGCN. r-AdjNorm~\citep{zhao2022radjnorm} shows that the normalisation
exponent of the adjacency controls how strongly popularity is amplified, and
tunes it; graph convolutions provably amplify popularity
bias~\citep{chen2024gcnpop}, and deconfounding can reduce the amplification of
user-level biases~\citep{wang2021decrs}. APDA~\citep{zhou2023apda} learns an adaptive inverse-popularity
weight per edge, CAGED~\citep{que2025caged} derives aggregation weights from a
backdoor adjustment, and DPAA~\citep{dpaa2026} learns interaction- and
layer-wise weights to counter the amplification of popularity by repeated
message passing. These methods change \emph{which} adjacency is propagated and
target popularity-related metrics. None of them analyses what happens to an
unbiased edge estimate when the re-weighted adjacency is multiplied with
itself over several hops. Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} shows that the product is
then biased, by a term of order $1/p$ that concentrates on rarely exposed
items, and DRUP removes this term exactly for any odd number of hops. Our linear
propagation baseline over the logged graph searches the normalisation exponent
and is therefore a training-free r-AdjNorm; we also train r-AdjNorm and NAVIP.

\paragraph{Unbiased estimation of polynomials under missingness}
The walk correction belongs to a classical idea: an unbiased estimator of a
polynomial in unknown quantities must avoid products of the same noisy
measurement, as in U-statistics~\citep{hoeffding1948}. For covariance matrices
estimated from Bernoulli-masked data, the off-diagonal entries of the masked
Gram matrix need a $1/p^2$ correction and the diagonal a $1/p$
correction~\citep{lounici2014}; our diagonal-corrected item operator $G$ is the
analogue for inverse-propensity and DR edges with heterogeneous propensities.
Unbiased subgraph counts from sampled edges~\citep{tsourakakis2009doulion},
and the passage from homomorphism counts to injective counts by Möbius
inversion on the partition lattice~\citep{rota1964}, are the combinatorial
counterparts of our $K$-hop construction. Removing walks that revisit vertices
or edges is also standard in network analysis: non-backtracking
operators~\citep{krzakala2013} and self-avoiding-walk
statistics~\citep{hopkins2017bayesian} discard backtracking walks to control
the spectrum or the variance. Our correction differs in that it keeps the
repeated walks and replaces each repeated factor by its unbiased
counterpart, so that the estimator targets the full walk sum, repeats
included. In recommendation, \citet{schnabel2020item} debias item-to-item
co-occurrence statistics with propensities and a small annotated sample. The
reduction itself is therefore not new. What is new is its combination with
inverse-propensity and DR edge estimates and degree weights in graph
recommendation, the exact bias of the uncorrected operators, the
public-operator form, and the consequences for exposure invariance. Our
treatment of nuisances follows double machine learning, whose cross-fitting we
adopt~\citep{chernozhukov2018dml}, and our clipping and control-variate choices
mirror doubly robust off-policy evaluation~\citep{dudik2011doubly}.

\paragraph{Fairness in recommendation}
Fairness notions in recommendation cover users and items, groups and
individuals, and outcomes and exposure~\citep{wang2023fairness}; a unifying
account of their measurement is given by~\citet{amigo2023fairness}, and
provider fairness and its relation to data characteristics have been analysed
in~\citet{gomez2022provider,deldjoo2021explaining}. On the item side, exposure can be allocated
in proportion to merit~\citep{singh2018exposure}, amortised over
time~\citep{biega2018equity} or under uncertainty about
merit~\citep{singh2021uncertainty}, and two-sided allocations can be guaranteed
by constrained matching~\citep{patro2020fairrec}; expected exposure evaluates
stochastic rankings against merit~\citep{diaz2020expected}, and
multi-stakeholder views weigh users, providers and the
platform~\citep{abdollahpouri2020multistakeholder,ekstrand2022fairness}.
Fairness can also be audited by counterfactual
reasoning~\citep{cornacchia2023auditing}. DRUP contributes a
causal item-side property of the merit \emph{estimate}, exposure invariance:
its expected scores do not respond to interventions on an item's exposure
(Theorem~\ref{thm:fair}). It is not a fairness criterion. Merit-based
allocation can be applied to any merit estimate, and what it requires is an
estimate whose errors do not depend on past exposure; exposure invariance is
one such property, relative to the full-exposure target $F^*$ as the notion of
merit. It can increase the concentration of recommendations, and the
distributive mechanism is an explicit allocation such as the exposure-capped
$b$-matching of Theorem~S5, related to~\citep{patro2020fairrec}.

\paragraph{Explanations, robustness and privacy}
Explainable recommendation is surveyed by~\citet{zhang2020explainable}. For graph models,
GNNExplainer~\citep{ying2019gnnexplainer} and CF-GNNExplainer~\citep{lucic2022cfgnn}
search for important or minimal sets of edges, PRINCE~\citep{ghazimatin2020prince}
finds minimal sets of a user's actions whose removal changes a random-walk
recommendation, and CountER~\citep{tan2021counter} optimises counterfactual
explanations of aspect-based models. Counterfactual explanations have a legal
motivation~\citep{wachter2018counterfactual} and hidden
assumptions~\citep{barocas2020hidden}, and Shapley attributions depend on their
baseline~\citep{kumar2020shapley}. Recommenders can be manipulated by injected
profiles~\citep{lam2004shilling,li2016poisoning,fang2018poisoning}, and shilling
groups can be detected with graph convolutions~\citep{wang2022shilling};
PORE~\citep{jia2023pore} certifies robustness by ensembling models trained on
sub-sampled data. Private recommenders perturb item--item
statistics~\citep{mcsherry2009netflix}, factorisation models~\citep{liu2024dpmf} or
matrix completion under joint differential privacy~\citep{jain2018dpmc}, and
federated approaches keep ratings on the device~\citep{chai2021fedmf}. Any
linear propagation operator with fixed nuisances, DRUP included, admits exact
removal attributions, closed-form certificates and a jointly private release
of its item operator with an analytically calibrated Gaussian
mechanism~\citep{balle2018analytic,hsu2014matchings}; we state and test these
properties in the Supplementary Material.

\section{Problem setting}
%======================================================================

Users $u\in[m]$, items $i\in[n]$ and pairs $e=(u,i)$ carry a binary potential
outcome $Y_e\in\{0,1\}$ (whether $u$ would like $i$ if it were shown) and an
observation indicator $O_e\in\{0,1\}$. The log reveals $O$ and $O\odot Y$. What
$O$ records depends on the platform: on KuaiRec it is an impression chosen by
the system, on Coat and Yahoo!\,R3 it is a rating that the user chose to give,
so the ``exposure'' mechanism modelled by $p$ is then user self-selection
rather than the platform's display policy. All statements below are relative
to the mechanism that generates $O$; we keep the word exposure for brevity.

\begin{assumption}[Conditionally independent exposure]\label{as:unconf}
Conditionally on $Y$, the exposures $O_e$ are independent Bernoulli variables
with propensities $p_e=\Pr(O_e=1\mid Y)>0$.
\end{assumption}
The propensity may depend on the pair's own outcome, so the log may be missing
not at random. All expectations below are over $O$ given $Y$.

\begin{assumption}[Fixed nuisances]\label{as:fixed}
The propensity estimates $\hat p$, the imputation $\hat Y\in[0,1]^{m\times n}$,
the edge weights $C$ and the constants $a,b$ of the score below are fixed
given $Y$, i.e., they do not depend on $O$ (for instance, they are fitted on
an independent log). We use $C_{ui}=d_u^{-\alpha}d_i^{-(1-\alpha)}$ with degrees
$d$ taken from $\hat Y$.
\end{assumption}
The weights $C$ also enter the target below, so with degrees from $\hat Y$ the
target is indexed by the imputation. A normalisation fixed a priori (a design
choice, or degrees from an independent source) makes the target independent
of $\hat Y$; Section~\ref{sec:semisynth} compares both.

\begin{remark}[Scope of the guarantees and the experimental protocol]\label{rem:xfit}
All guarantees below are stated under Assumption~\ref{as:fixed}. Our main
experimental protocol does not satisfy it, in four ways. (i) $\hat p$ and
$\hat Y$ are cross-fitted over ten random folds of pairs: the nuisances used at
a pair of fold $k$ are fitted on the other nine
folds~\citep{chernozhukov2018dml}. This makes the nuisance at a pair
independent of that pair's exposure, which is what a one-hop estimate needs,
but not of the exposures of the other edges of a walk, so a product of
several edge estimates can be biased although every single edge estimate is
unbiased. (ii) The degree weights sum the imputation over all pairs of a user
or an item, so they depend on the exposures of all these pairs, including the
pair at which they are used. (iii) One of the searched degree sources takes
degrees from edge estimates. (iv) The constants $a,b$ are computed from the
scores (Section~\ref{sec:drup}). In simulation, the resulting dependence bias
is of the same order as the repeated-walk bias, and under this protocol with
imputation-based degrees the corrected operator is not less biased than the
uncorrected one
(Section~\ref{sec:mc}); a control in which only the degree weights are fixed a
priori restores the correction under cross-fitting, so (ii) and (iii), more than (i),
account for the failure. The theorems therefore describe DRUP with nuisances
from an independent log, or from the sample split introduced below; for the
cross-fitted protocol they give no guarantee, and our experiments with it
evaluate accuracy and the empirical behaviour of the scores, not unbiasedness.
\end{remark}

With $\tY=C\odot Y$, the target is propagation over the full-exposure graph,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:target}
F^*=a\,\tY+b\,T^*,\qquad T^*=\tY\tY^\top\tY ,
\end{equation}
with fixed constants $a,b\ge0$. $F^*$ is the linear LightGCN score one would
compute if every item had been shown to every user. It is a normative choice:
it treats potential outcomes as fixed and ignores preferences that exposure
itself shapes~\citep{chaney2018confounding,perdomo2020performative}
(Section~\ref{sec:disc}).

\section{DRUP}\label{sec:drup}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{Edge estimates}
With clipped propensities $\bar p=\max(\hat p,\tau)$,
\begin{equation}
W^{\mathrm{IPS}}_e=\frac{O_eY_e}{\bar p_e},\qquad
W^{\mathrm{DR}}_e=\hat Y_e+\frac{O_e(Y_e-\hat Y_e)}{\bar p_e}.
\end{equation}
Both have mean $Y_e$ when $\bar p=p$.

\paragraph{Where the plug-in operator fails}
The plug-in three-hop operator $(\tW\tW^\top\tW)_{ui}$, with $\tW=C\odot W$, sums
the products $\tW_{uj}\tW_{vj}\tW_{vi}$ over all walks $u\!-\!j\!-\!v\!-\!i$.
If $v\neq u$ and $j\neq i$ the three edges are distinct and, by independence,
the product is unbiased. The other walks repeat an edge
(Figure~\ref{fig:walks}): for $v=u$ the edge $(u,j)$ is traversed twice, for
$j=i$ the edge $(v,i)$, and for $v=u,j=i$ the edge $(u,i)$ three times. A
repeated factor $\tW_e^k$ has mean $C_e^k\E[W_e^k]\ne C_e^kY_e$; for IPS,
$\E[W_e^2]=Y_e/p_e$. Because $Y_e^k=Y_e$ for binary outcomes, the unbiased
replacement of $\tW_e^k$ is simply $C_e^kW_e$.

\paragraph{Idempotent walk correction}
Let $\Delta=\tW\odot\tW-C\odot C\odot W$, with row sums $r$ and column sums
$\kappa$. The corrected three-hop operator subtracts the excess of each walk
type:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:drup}
\hat T=\tW\tW^\top\tW-\tW\odot(r\mathbf 1^\top-\Delta)-\tW\odot(\mathbf 1\kappa^\top-\Delta)
-(\tW^{\odot3}-C^{\odot3}\odot W).
\end{equation}
The second term removes the excess of type (b) walks, the third that of type
(c), and the last that of type (d); the subtracted $\Delta$ inside the first
two avoids counting type (d) twice. The DRUP score is $s=a\,\tW+b\,\hat T$.
In the experiments $a=1/c_1$ and $b=\beta/c_3$, where $c_1$ and $c_3$ are the
mean absolute values of the one- and three-hop terms over all scored users and
$\beta$ is selected on validation data. The constants are global, so every
user's scores are the same linear combination of the two terms, and
within-user rankings depend on them only through $b/a$; a data-dependent
$c_3/c_1$ thus acts as a data-dependent rescaling of the grid of $\beta$.
Because $c_1$ and $c_3$ are computed from the log, however,
$\E[\hat T/c_3]\neq T^*/c_3$ in general, and the score-level statements below
hold for fixed $a,b$. Constants computed from the imputed graph $C\odot\hat Y$
satisfy Assumption~\ref{as:fixed} whenever $\hat Y$ does; Section~\ref{sec:mc}
compares both choices.

\paragraph{Sample-split variant}
A log rarely comes with an independent log for its nuisances. A sample split
provides one at a cost. Draw a random set $A$ of pairs, each pair with
probability $q$, fit $\hat Y$ (and $\hat p$, where it is estimated) on the
pairs in $A$ only, replace
the edge estimates of the pairs in $A$ by $\hat Y$, take the degrees from
$\hat Y$, and compute $c_1$ and $c_3$ on the imputed graph $C\odot\hat Y$.
Conditionally on $A$ and on the exposures in $A$, the remaining edge estimates
are independent of every nuisance, so Assumption~\ref{as:fixed} holds and the
results below apply, with $\xi_e=\hat Y_e-Y_e$ on the pairs in $A$. The price
is a first-order imputation bias on a fraction $q$ of the pairs and fewer
exposures for the edge estimates. We call this variant DRUP-split and use
$q=0.2$. The guarantee holds for every fixed configuration; selecting the
hyper-parameters on validation data, as every method in our comparison does,
is outside it, as it is for any estimator.

\paragraph{Public-operator form}
Define the diagonal-corrected item Gram matrix
$G=\tW^\top\tW-\diag(\tW^\top\tW)+\diag(\mathbf 1^\top(C\odot C\odot W))$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:local}
\hat T_{u\cdot}=\tilde w_uG-\tilde w_u\odot(r_u\mathbf 1^\top-2\zeta_u)-(\tilde w_u^{\odot3}-c_u^{\odot3}\odot w_u),
\end{equation}
where $\zeta_u=\tilde w_u^{\odot2}-c_u^{\odot2}\odot w_u$ and lower-case rows
belong to user $u$. Equation~\eqref{eq:local} matches~\eqref{eq:drup} to machine
precision. $G$ is the only object shared across users
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:drup}). Its off-diagonal entries are products of distinct
edges; its diagonal is corrected from $\sum_u\tW_{ui}^2$ to
$\sum_uC_{ui}^2W_{ui}$, the analogue of the diagonal correction of a Gram
matrix estimated from masked data~\citep{lounici2014}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{DRUP (training-free)}\label{alg:drup}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require log $(O,O\odot Y)$; hyper-parameters $\tau,\alpha,\lambda,\beta$
\State fit $\hat p$ (logistic exposure model) and $\hat Y$ (IPS-weighted additive ridge, optionally plus an IPS-weighted low-rank ALS residual), cross-fitted over ten folds of pairs
\State $\bar p\gets\max(\hat p,\tau)$; \ $W\gets\hat Y+O\odot(Y-\hat Y)/\bar p$
\State $C\gets d_u^{-\alpha}d_i^{-(1-\alpha)}$ with $d$ from $\hat Y$
\State $G\gets\tW^\top\tW-\diag(\tW^\top\tW)+\diag(\mathbf 1^\top(C\odot C\odot W))$ \Comment{public operator}
\For{each user $u$ (locally)}
\State $\hat T_{u\cdot}\gets$ Eq.~\eqref{eq:local}; \ $s_u\gets a\,\tilde w_u+b\,\hat T_{u\cdot}$; rank unexposed items by $s_u$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Control-variate family}
More generally, $W^\lambda_e=O_eY_e/\bar p_e+\lambda(\hat Y_e-O_e\hat Y_e/\bar p_e)$
is unbiased for every $\lambda\in[0,1]$ under correct propensities. It
interpolates between IPS ($\lambda=0$) and DR ($\lambda=1$, the only member that
is also robust to propensity errors), and it equals the DR estimate with the
shrunk imputation $\lambda\hat Y$. With $\bar p=p$ its variance is
$\frac{1-p}{p}(Y-\lambda\hat Y)^2$. All results below hold for every member, and
$\lambda$ is selected on validation data.

\paragraph{Imputation}
Theorem~\ref{thm:unbiased} holds for any fixed $\hat Y$, so the imputation is a
design choice; its accuracy enters the variance and the clipping bias
(Theorem~\ref{thm:var}). We use an additive model $\mu+b_u+b_i$ fitted by
IPS-weighted ridge regression, or that model plus a rank-32 residual $UV^\top$
fitted by IPS-weighted alternating least squares. The additive imputation gives
every user the same item order on unexposed candidates, which makes the one-hop
DR term non-personalised; the low-rank imputation removes this limitation.

\paragraph{Complexity and scale}
At three hops, one $n\times n$ Gram product and $O(mn)$ reductions, i.e., the
cost of the uncorrected operator. The $K$-hop correction of
Theorem~\ref{thm:khop} evaluates one tensor contraction per pair of equality
patterns; we report its measured cost for $K=5$ in Section~\ref{sec:mc}. On KuaiRec (7{,}176 users, 10{,}728 items) one
configuration takes a few seconds on four CPU cores once the nuisances are
fitted. The dense $W$ and $G$
need $O(mn+n^2)$ memory, which limits the present implementation to catalogues
of about $10^4$--$10^5$ items. Because the correction only modifies diagonals
and row and column sums, it carries over to sparse or low-rank
representations of $W$, but we have not evaluated catalogues of $10^6$ items.

\section{Theory}

Proofs are in \ref{app:proofs}. Let $\varepsilon_e=|Y_e-\hat Y_e|$.

\subsection{Unbiasedness and its limits}

\begin{theorem}[Edge-wise double robustness]\label{thm:unbiased}
Under Assumptions~\ref{as:unconf}--\ref{as:fixed}, let
$\xi_e=(p_e/\bar p_e-1)(Y_e-\hat Y_e)$. Then $\E[\hat T_{ui}]$ equals $T^*_{ui}$
with every distinct edge contributing $Y_e+\xi_e$ once. In particular, if
every edge has a correct propensity or a correct imputation, then
$\E[s]=F^*$.
\end{theorem}

Recommendations rank the \emph{unexposed} candidates of a user, and there
the candidate's own edge estimate is deterministic ($W_{ui}=\hat Y_{ui}$ for DR,
$0$ for IPS). The following corollary states what DRUP estimates on the
candidate set.

\begin{corollary}[Unexposed candidates]\label{cor:cand}
Under the conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:unbiased} with $\xi\equiv0$,
\begin{gather*}
\E[s_{ui}\mid O_{ui}=0]=F^*_{ui}-(Y_{ui}-\hat Y_{ui})\,g_{ui},\\
g_{ui}=\frac{\partial F^*_{ui}}{\partial Y_{ui}}
=C_{ui}\Big(a+b\big(\textstyle\sum_{v\neq u}C_{vi}^2Y_{vi}+\sum_{j\neq i}C_{uj}^2Y_{uj}+C_{ui}^2\big)\Big)\ge0 .
\end{gather*}
For IPS edges the same holds with $\hat Y_{ui}=0$. The residual is the
imputation error at the candidate itself. If $p_e$ depends on $Y$ only through
$Y_e$, the log carries no information on $Y_{ui}$ when $O_{ui}=0$, so the
residual is not identified and no estimator can remove it.
\end{corollary}

At three hops the walks through $(u,i)$ are exactly those counted in
$g_{ui}$. With IPS edges they vanish on unexposed candidates, so the uncorrected
IPS operator has no repeated-walk bias there; from five hops on, walks repeat
edges that are not incident to the candidate (for example
$u\!-\!j\!-\!v\!-\!j\!-\!v\!-\!i$) and the bias reappears
(Table~\ref{tab:mc}).

\begin{theorem}[Any odd number of hops]\label{thm:khop}
For $K=2r+1$, a walk $U_0\!-\!J_1\!-\!U_1\!-\cdots-\!U_r\!-\!J_{r+1}$ has user
variables $U_0,\dots,U_r$ and item variables $J_1,\dots,J_{r+1}$, with $U_0=u$
and $J_{r+1}=i$. The equality pattern of an assignment is a pair $\pi$ of set
partitions of the user and item variables, and $\pi$ fixes the multiplicity
$k_e$ of every distinct edge. Let $g_\pi(\sigma)$ be the unconstrained
contraction, over the blocks of a coarser pattern $\sigma\ge\pi$, of
$\prod_eC_e^{k_e}W_e$ (corrected) or of $\prod_e\tW_e^{k_e}$ (plug-in), the
product running over the distinct edges of $\pi$. Then
\[
\hat T^{(K)}=\tW(\tW^\top\tW)^r+\sum_{\pi:\,\exists k_e>1}\ \sum_{\sigma\ge\pi}\mu(\pi,\sigma)\big(g^{\mathrm{corr}}_\pi(\sigma)-g^{\mathrm{plug}}_\pi(\sigma)\big),
\]
with $\mu$ the Möbius function of the product of the two partition lattices,
satisfies $\E\hat T^{(K)}=(\tY\tY^\top)^r\tY$ under the conditions of
Theorem~\ref{thm:unbiased} with $\xi\equiv0$.
\end{theorem}
The number of pairs $(\pi,\sigma)$ is 5, 99 and 2{,}790 for $K=3,5,7$, and for
$K=3$ the construction reduces to Eq.~\eqref{eq:drup}. Theorem~\ref{thm:unbiased},
Corollary~\ref{cor:cand} and Theorem~\ref{thm:fair} carry over to any odd $K$.

Assumption~\ref{as:unconf} excludes slates of fixed size, where exposures in a
user's history are negatively dependent (Coat, for instance, collects exactly
24 ratings per user). The next result bounds the resulting bias.

\begin{proposition}[Exposure dependence within users]\label{prop:dep}
Suppose that exposures are independent across users but may be dependent within
a user's row, with $|\mathrm{Cov}(O_e,O_f\mid Y)|\le\rho\,p_ep_f$ for distinct $e,f$ in
the same row. With correct propensities,
\[
\big|\E\hat T_{ui}-T^*_{ui}\big|\ \le\ \rho\sum_{j\neq i}C_{uj}\Big(\sum_{v\neq u}C_{vj}C_{vi}\,\varepsilon_{vj}\varepsilon_{vi}+C_{uj}C_{ui}\,\varepsilon_{uj}\varepsilon_{ui}\Big).
\]
For slates of $k$ items drawn uniformly without replacement, $\rho\le1/k$, and
$\rho\le1$ for any negatively dependent design. For DR edges the bias is of
second order in the imputation error; for IPS edges ($\varepsilon=Y$) it is of
first order.
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}[Repeated-walk bias of uncorrected propagation]\label{thm:lower}
Let the propensities be correct and let $(u,i)$ be a candidate with $O_{ui}=0$.
Relative to the conditional target of Corollary~\ref{cor:cand}, the
uncorrected DR operator $\tW\tW^\top\tW$ has conditional bias
\begin{gather*}
C_{ui}\hat Y_{ui}\big(R_u^{(i)}+K_i\big)-C_{ui}^3\hat Y_{ui}(1-\hat Y_{ui}^2),\\
K_i=\sum_{v\neq u}C_{vi}^2V_{vi},\qquad R_u^{(i)}=\sum_{j\neq i}C_{uj}^2V_{uj},
\end{gather*}
where $V_e=\Var(W_e)=\frac{1-p_e}{p_e}\varepsilon_e^2$. Moreover:
(a) $K_i\ge(1/\max_vp_{vi}-1)\sum_{v\neq u}C_{vi}^2\varepsilon_{vi}^2$, which is unbounded
as the item's propensities vanish;
(b) with clipped propensities $\bar p=\max(p,\tau)$, $\tau\le1/2$, the excess
$C_e^2(\E[W_e^2]-\E[W_e])$ of a doubly traversed edge is at most
$C_e^2(1/\tau-1)\varepsilon_e^2$, with equality at $p_e=\tau$; the worst-case
repeated-walk bias is therefore $\Theta(1/\tau)$;
(c) for IPS edges the conditional bias vanishes at three hops.
DRUP has zero bias relative to the same target.
\end{theorem}
The within-user ranking is affected through $C_{ui}\hat Y_{ui}K_i$, which
up-weights items whose other raters were rarely exposed; $R_u^{(i)}$ depends on
$i$ only through one term.

\begin{theorem}[Variance, clipping and concentration]\label{thm:var}
Let $\Lambda_e(u,i)$ be the $C$-weighted mass of the walks from $u$ to $i$ through
edge $e$, and $B=\max(1,1/\tau)$. Then
(a) $\Var(W_e)\le\varepsilon_e^2/(4\tau^2)$, and $\Var(W_e)\le\varepsilon_e^2/\tau$ if $\hat p=p$;
(b) if $\hat p=p$, $\Var(\hat T_{ui})\le\frac{(1+1/\tau)^2}{\tau}\sum_e\varepsilon_e^2\Lambda_e(u,i)^2$;
(c) if $\hat p=p$, so that clipping is the only source of bias,
$|\E\hat T_{ui}-T^*_{ui}|\le3\bar\xi(1+\bar\xi)^2\sum_{j,v}C_{uj}C_{vj}C_{vi}$ with
$\bar\xi=\max_e(1-p_e/\tau)_+\varepsilon_e$;
(d) for any $\hat p$, $\Pr(|\hat T_{ui}-\E\hat T_{ui}|\ge t)\le2\exp(-2t^2/\sum_ec_e^2)$ with
$c_e=\varepsilon_eB^2\Lambda_e(u,i)/\tau$.
\end{theorem}

The variance scales with the imputation error $\varepsilon^2$, not with $Y^2$ as
for IPS, and $\tau$ trades variance against a clipping bias that vanishes when
$\hat Y$ is accurate. The constants are worst-case and loose; Section~\ref{sec:mc}
compares bound (b) with the Monte-Carlo variance.

\begin{definition}[Exposure elasticity]\label{def:elast}
For an intervention $do(p_{\cdot i}\leftarrow\pi p_{\cdot i})$ on item $i$'s
exposure mechanism, after which the estimator uses the post-intervention
propensities ($\hat p_{\cdot i}\leftarrow\pi\hat p_{\cdot i}$) and otherwise the same
nuisances, $\eta_i=\partial\log\E[s_{ui}]/\partial\log\pi$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Exposure invariance]\label{thm:fair}
Under Assumptions~\ref{as:unconf}--\ref{as:fixed} and $\pi p\ge\tau$, DRUP has
$\eta_i=0$, also conditionally on $O_{ui}=0$: the expected score of every
candidate is unchanged when item $i$'s exposure mechanism is scaled.
Logged-graph propagation with fixed weights $C$ has $\eta_i\ge1$ (popularity
amplification); normalising by logged degrees partly offsets it.
Uncorrected DR propagation has $\eta_i<0$ whenever $\hat Y_{ui}K_i>0$
(over-correction).
\end{theorem}

Exposure invariance is a statement about expected scores. It is not a
distributive fairness criterion, and it is conditional: when many propensities
lie below the clip, $\pi p\ge\tau$ fails and invariance is lost
(Section~\ref{sec:fair}).

\subsection{Structural properties}\label{sec:structsum}

Given its nuisances, $s$ is a fixed linear function of the edge estimates.
Section~S1 of the Supplementary Material states and proves the
consequences, which hold for every linear propagation operator, corrected or
not: exact attributions and minimal counterfactual explanations with frozen
nuisances (Proposition~S1, Theorem~S2), conservative
certificates against injected profiles (Theorem~S3), joint
differential privacy when the population-level nuisances, the
hyper-parameters and the score constants are public (Theorem~S4),
and an exposure-capped allocation solved exactly by min-cost flow
(Theorem~S5). Because they are not specific to DRUP, we report
their empirical behaviour in the Supplementary Material
(Section~S2) and use only the allocation in the main text, as a
decision that compares scores across users (Section~\ref{sec:semisynth}).

\subsection{Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Data}
\emph{Coat}~\citep{schnabel2016} has 290 users, 300 items and 6{,}960 self-selected
(MNAR) ratings, exactly 24 per user. The test data are 16 ratings per user on
uniformly random items (MAR); we rank only the items the user did not rate,
which leaves 14.7 candidates per user on average, and 225 users have at least
one positive candidate. The dataset ships propensities, which we use.
\emph{Yahoo!\,R3}~\citep{marlin2009collaborative} has 311{,}704 self-selected ratings of
15{,}400 users on 1{,}000 songs (2.0\% dense) and 54{,}000 ratings of 10 random
songs for 5{,}400 of the users, in the version distributed
with~\citet{chen2021autodebias}. A random 5\% of the MAR users is held out and used
only to fit an outcome-dependent Naive-Bayes propensity~\citep{schnabel2016}; in a
preliminary comparison on validation data it did not improve the propagation
operators, and all reported results use the logistic exposure model. The remaining 5{,}130 test users have 10 candidates each, and 2{,}315 of
them have a positive. \emph{KuaiRec}~\citep{gao2022kuairec} provides the big matrix
(7{,}176 users, 10{,}728 items, 10.3M logged views, 13.4\% dense) as the MNAR log and
a fully observed small matrix (1{,}411 users, 3{,}327 items) as the test set. We rank
all small-matrix items that the user never viewed in the log (3{,}314 candidates
per user, 154 of them positive on average). \emph{KuaiRand-Pure}~\citep{gao2022kuairand}
is the only one of the four datasets whose log records platform exposure rather
than self-selection: $O$ is an impression of the standard recommendation feed and
$Y$ a click. We keep a random 10{,}000 of the users who also received randomly
exposed videos, and all 7{,}583 videos; the standard log then has 511{,}863
impressions (0.68\% dense, click rate 47\%). The random impressions of the same
period, restricted to videos the user was not shown in the standard log, are
the test set (43.6 candidates per user, 17.5\% clicked; 8{,}347 users have a
positive). Positives are ratings $\ge4$ (Coat, Yahoo!\,R3), a watch ratio $\ge2$
(KuaiRec) and a click (KuaiRand). A random ranking reaches nDCG@5 of 0.306 on
Coat and 0.352 on Yahoo!\,R3 and nDCG@20 of 0.046 on KuaiRec. The candidate sets
of Coat and Yahoo!\,R3 are small, so their metric differences are compressed;
KuaiRec ranks thousands of candidates. We report nDCG@5 on Coat and
Yahoo!\,R3, nDCG@10 on KuaiRand and nDCG@20 on KuaiRec, and AUC and Recall in
the Supplementary Material. On Coat and Yahoo!\,R3 the log is self-selected
ratings: ``exposure'' there means that the user chose to rate the item, and
exposure invariance is invariance to that choice, not to a platform's
allocation.

\paragraph{Protocol}
The unbiased data are split into a validation part (30\% of each user's
candidates on Coat, 30\% of the test users otherwise) and a test part, over 10
(Coat) or 5 random splits. Every method selects its configuration on the
validation part of each split, and trained models also select their epoch
there: each split stops on its own validation score and never sees the
validation or test users of another split. All fitted nuisances are cross-fitted over ten folds of pairs, except
for the sample-split variants, whose imputation (and propensity model, where
it is estimated) is fitted on a random 20\% of the pairs; only the latter satisfy the assumptions of the theory
(Remark~\ref{rem:xfit}). We report the mean and standard deviation over splits
of the test metric. Because paired tests over re-sampled splits are anti-conservative when the
splits overlap~\citep{nadeau2003}, differences are tested at the level
of users, with a Holm correction over all comparisons of a dataset
(\ref{app:details}). These tests treat the training log, the fitted
nuisances and the trained models as fixed: they account for the variation
over test users, not for re-drawing the log or re-fitting the pipeline, which
we assess only through the split and seed standard deviations. Progress claims in top-$N$ recommendation have
repeatedly failed against well-tuned simple
baselines~\citep{dacrema2019progress,rendle2019baselines,anelli2023myth}; we therefore
tune every method on the same validation data and report how many
configurations each one searched.

\paragraph{Methods}
We compare DRUP with up to 26 baselines (22 on KuaiRand, where eleven models were trained). \emph{Non-personalised}: popularity and the imputation
$\hat Y$ alone. \emph{Trained with a pointwise loss}: MF, IPS-MF~\citep{schnabel2016},
DR-MF, DR-JL~\citep{wang2019drjl}, MRDR~\citep{guo2021mrdr}, iALS~\citep{hu2008ials},
LightGCN and DR-LightGCN (LightGCN with the DR loss).
\emph{Trained with a pairwise or contrastive loss}: MF, PDA~\citep{zhang2021pda},
MACR~\citep{wei2021macr}, LightGCN~\citep{he2020lightgcn}, r-AdjNorm~\citep{zhao2022radjnorm},
NAVIP~\citep{kim2022navip} and SimGCL~\citep{yu2022simgcl}, all with BPR.
\emph{Training-free on the logged graph}: linear LightGCN
(the one- plus three-hop form of Section~\ref{sec:drup} on $O\odot Y$; with the searched
normalisation exponent it is a linear r-AdjNorm), EASE~\citep{steck2019ease},
GF-CF~\citep{shen2021gfcf} and BSPM~\citep{choi2023bspm}. \emph{Training-free on a debiased graph}: IPS adjacency
(NAVIP-style), IPS adjacency with the walk correction, EASE, GF-CF and BSPM on the DR
graph $W$, the uncorrected DR adjacency, and the five-hop DR adjacency. Besides
DRUP we report its five-hop version and DR adjacency and DRUP with the sample
split of Section~\ref{sec:drup} (DRUP-split), over five draws of the split-off
pairs. Grids, the number of configurations per method, and training details are
in \ref{app:details}. The training-free methods search between 1 and
6{,}300 configurations and the trained ones between 6 and 108. Trained models
are evaluated after every epoch; each split keeps the best (configuration,
epoch) on its own validation part and stops once its validation score has not
improved for a fixed number of epochs.

experiments this advantage disappears: the corrected operator is biased by
0.17--0.80 and the uncorrected one by 0.11--0.61, because the nuisances, and in
particular the degree weights, depend on the exposures of the walk edges, and
this dependence partly offsets the repeated-walk excess of the uncorrected
operator. The dependence bias shrinks as users have more exposures (from 0.80
at 11 to 0.18 at 60 per user) but does not vanish. A control that fixes the degree
weights a priori (Table~S16 in the Supplementary Material)
isolates the cause: with fixed weights, cross-fitting the propensity and the imputation
alone preserves the correction (IPS with the correction 0.05 against 1.10 uncorrected;
DRUP 0.05 against 0.89), whereas degrees taken from the cross-fitted $\hat Y$ or from
the edge estimates destroy it (0.64--0.80 and 0.74--0.82). The failure of the main protocol is thus
due to log-dependent degree weights, not to cross-fitting as such; fitting the
nuisances in sample, without cross-fitting, breaks the correction even with fixed
weights for DR edges (0.23). Degrees from edge estimates
make the uncorrected operators much worse at low exposure (up to 4.1). The sample split
restores the guarantee: relative to the target of
Theorem~\ref{thm:unbiased} with $\hat Y$ on the imputed pairs, the bias of
DRUP-split is 0.007--0.065, and relative to the full-exposure target it is
0.07--0.18 with $q=0.2$, against 0.07--1.09 for the uncorrected operator under the same
split; $q=0.5$ imputes too many pairs (0.13--0.42). The data-dependent
constants matter as well: with independent nuisances and DR edges, the full
score is biased by 0.18--0.35 when $c_1,c_3$ are computed from the scores and by
0.02--0.11 when they come from the imputed graph. On
unexposed candidates, the conditional target of Corollary~\ref{cor:cand}
differs from the full-exposure target by 12\% (three hops) and 6\% (five hops)
of the mean target in this model, and it agrees with it in within-user order
only up to Kendall $\tau=0.63$ and 0.77: this is the part of $F^*$ that no
estimator can recover on the candidates actually ranked. Results with
estimated propensities, which are misspecified in this model and bias every
operator, are in Table~S6 of the Supplementary Material.

Stress tests outside the assumptions of Theorem~\ref{thm:unbiased} are in
the Supplementary Material (Table~S5). With fixed-size slates, which
make exposures within a user negatively dependent, IPS picks up a first-order
bias (0.22) whereas DRUP stays at 0.024, as Proposition~\ref{prop:dep} predicts;
with misspecified propensities DRUP is exactly unbiased when the imputation is
exact and biased by 0.16 when it is noisy, against 0.57 for IPS and 1.9 for the
uncorrected DR operator. The bounds of Theorem~\ref{thm:var} were never violated
but are loose by a factor of 70 to 8{,}000 (Table~S7).

The real benchmarks cannot show whether score values are right, because
$F^*$ is unknown. We therefore use KuaiRec's fully observed small matrix
(1{,}411 users, 3{,}327 items, 4.6\% positives) as the potential outcomes $Y$ and
draw exposure logs from a known MNAR mechanism fitted to the big matrix,
$\operatorname{logit}p_{ui}=c+z_u+1.5z_i+Y_{ui}$, with $z$ the standardised logit
exposure rates of the user and the item in the big log and $c$ set for a
density of 13\% as in the big matrix (median propensity 0.064, all
propensities at least $\tau=0.02$). The target uses weights $C$ fixed a priori
(degrees of $Y$, $\alpha=0.5$) and $a,b$ fixed from the target. Each estimator
uses either the same fixed $C$ or degrees from its imputation (as in our
real-data configurations), a rank-16 IPS-weighted imputation, and nuisances
from an independent log, cross-fitted on the same log, or from the 20\% sample
split. Table~\ref{tab:semisynth} reports score errors and three uses of the
scores.

\paragraph{Score values} With true propensities and fixed weights, the
correction lowers the relative RMSE of the three-hop term by a factor of 4 to
12 (IPS 22.8 to 2.9, DR 28.2 to 6.3 with an independent log; DR 35.3 to 8.5
cross-fitted): the repeated factors of rarely exposed edges dominate the error
as well as the bias. With 30 draws the remaining three-hop bias of the
corrected operators is within Monte-Carlo error; at five hops the relative
bias drops from 3.46 (DR) to 0.32 (DRUP). The difference is visible in the
quantity a score-level user cares about. Summing the scores of a fixed
allocation estimates its full-exposure utility, and the uncorrected operators
overestimate it by 18--29\% (random allocation) and 10--19\% (popular items),
whereas IPS with the correction and DRUP are within 2\% of the truth. This
holds with an independent log and, because $C$ is fixed, also with
cross-fitted nuisances (DRUP $-0.1\pm1.6\%$ against DR $+28.6\pm4.7\%$). The
sample split removes the dependence but imputes a fifth of the pairs, and its
imputation bias shifts the corrected estimates up by 38--47\% (uncorrected:
53--64\%).

\paragraph{What does not change} Two uses of the scores are unaffected. The
exposure-capped allocation, which compares scores across users, loses 79--84\%
of the achievable full-exposure utility under every estimator: per-pair noise,
not the repeated-walk bias, decides which pairs are allocated. Rankings do not
improve either: with imputation-based weights, DR adjacency and DRUP reach
nDCG@20 of 0.377 and 0.370. With weights taken from the imputation, finally,
every operator estimates a different, imputation-indexed quantity; with an
independent log or cross-fitted nuisances it is off by 40--57\% in utility
against the fixed-weight target, corrected or not, so score values need
weights fixed a priori. With estimated propensities all estimators
overstate utility by 100--540\%, and the propensity model, not the walk
correction, dominates the error (Table~S12).

\paragraph{A sparser log} At a density of 3\% (median propensity 0.008, 58\% of
the propensities below the clip $\tau=0.01$) the conditions of the theorems
fail, and clipping dominates (Table~S13). The correction
still lowers the three-hop RMSE by a factor of 7 to 32, but every utility
estimate is biased: IPS with the correction by $-28\%$, DR adjacency by $+331\%$
and DRUP by $+164\%$ with an independent log, in line with the clipping bias of
Theorem~\ref{thm:var}(c).

Tables~\ref{tab:acc} and~\ref{tab:sig} report accuracy and the user-level
tests; AUC and Recall are in the Supplementary Material and lead to the same
conclusions.

\paragraph{The walk correction does not change accuracy} On every dataset DRUP
and the uncorrected DR adjacency with the same grid are not significantly
different (Coat $+0.0014$, KuaiRec $-0.0001$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=1$) and are equivalent within
$\pm0.005$ nDCG by two one-sided tests ($p_{\mathrm{TOST}}<10^{-3}$ on Yahoo!\,R3, KuaiRand and KuaiRec,
0.034 on Coat; Table~S17), and on Yahoo!\,R3 and KuaiRand they coincide exactly, because
validation selects the IPS end of the control-variate family ($\lambda=0$) on
every split and IPS edges have no three-hop repeated-walk bias on unexposed
candidates (Corollary~\ref{cor:cand}); on KuaiRand the selected clip is the smallest in the grid, 0.02, on all splits. The IPS adjacency and IPS with the
correction give identical rankings on all datasets for the same reason. At five
hops, where the correction is active for both edge types, the corrected
operator is lower by 0.0003 nDCG@20 on KuaiRec ($p=0.011$ before and
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.16$ after the adjustment) and by 0.008 nDCG@5 on Coat
($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=1$). The same holds under the sample split (Coat $-0.003$,
Yahoo!\,R3 $-0.001$, KuaiRec $-0.0001$; all $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=1$). Five hops do not add
accuracy over three (KuaiRec 0.6376 against 0.6378).

\paragraph{The sample split} DRUP-split, the only variant covered by the
guarantees, costs little on the larger and denser logs and much on the sparse
one. On KuaiRec it reaches $0.6321\pm0.0035$ over five draws of the split-off pairs
(0.627 to 0.636), 0.006 below the cross-fitted DRUP. For the first draw (0.6337) the
gap is 0.0035 ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.003$), and that draw remains
significantly above pointwise LightGCN ($+0.007$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<10^{-3}$; Wilcoxon
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.48$) and on par with DR-JL and MRDR ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=1$). 
On Coat its accuracy depends on the draw ($0.544\pm0.011$, from 0.527 to 0.558):
with 24 logged ratings per user, which fifth of them is spent on the nuisances
matters, and the first draw is not significantly different from the
cross-fitted DRUP ($+0.009$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=1$). On Yahoo!\,R3 it loses 0.062
($0.595\pm0.004$; $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<10^{-3}$ for the first draw), because a fifth of the
few logged exposures goes to the nuisances and the imputation that replaces
them is poor. Section~\ref{sec:semisynth} shows the corresponding cost for
score values: the imputed pairs bias the utility estimates upwards.

\paragraph{Propagation over the DR graph is what helps on KuaiRec} On KuaiRec,
whose test matrix is fully observed and whose candidate sets have 3{,}314 items,
training-free propagation over the DR graph reaches nDCG@20 of 0.638 and is
significantly better than every trained model at the user level: the best are the jointly
learned doubly robust models DR-JL and MRDR (0.633; $+0.004$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.003$; with the
Nadeau--Bengio test over splits the margin over DR-JL is not significant, $p=0.14$, and over MRDR $p=0.006$, and it is smaller than their seed spread of 0.009 and 0.015),
followed by pointwise LightGCN (0.627; $+0.010$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<10^{-3}$). It is also
better than every operator on the logged graph (GF-CF 0.614, BSPM 0.614, linear
LightGCN 0.604) and than the imputation used alone (0.619). GF-CF on the same DR
graph performs as well as DRUP (0.637, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=1$), so the gain comes from
the DR graph and not from a particular filter. A control that propagates the imputation alone
(every edge is $\hat Y$, no observed residual) reaches 0.6312: about two thirds of the
0.018 that DRUP gains over the imputation (0.6194) come from propagating the
imputation, and one third from the observed residuals. On Coat the control equals
the imputation (0.553 against 0.553; DRUP 0.547), and on Yahoo!\,R3 it is far below DRUP
(0.528 against 0.657), where the IPS edges carry the signal.

\paragraph{KuaiRand} KuaiRand is the only dataset whose log records platform
exposure. Its log is 0.7\% dense, and as on Yahoo!\,R3 validation selects IPS
edges ($\lambda=0$) on every split, so DRUP, DR adjacency and IPS adjacency
coincide (nDCG@10 0.4492). They are not significantly different from GF-CF
(0.4502) or BSPM (0.4498) on the logged graph, from iALS (0.4528) or from
BPR-trained MF (0.4475; all $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=1$), and they are significantly
behind the BPR graph models: r-AdjNorm (0.4590; $-0.010$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<10^{-3}$), LightGCN
(0.4569; $-0.008$) and NAVIP (0.4565; $-0.008$; both $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<10^{-3}$). They
are significantly ahead of popularity (0.4204), of the pointwise models (LightGCN
0.406, MF 0.371, DR-JL 0.376), of MACR (0.431) and of SimGCL (0.420). The sample split
costs 0.009 ($0.4400\pm0.0007$ over five draws; $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<10^{-3}$), with
DRUP-split and DR adjacency under the split again indistinguishable
($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=1$). Only eleven baselines were trained on KuaiRand.

\paragraph{Coat and Yahoo!\,R3} On Coat (290 users, 15 candidates per user) the
most accurate method is iALS (0.581), followed by BSPM on the logged graph,
DR-LightGCN and the BPR graph models (0.566--0.573); DRUP reaches 0.547. With the Holm
adjustment over all 124 comparisons, DRUP is significantly better than the
pointwise MF, IPS-MF, DR-JL and MRDR and not significantly different from any
other method, including iALS ($-0.037$, $p=0.033$ before adjustment) and GF-CF on
the DR graph ($-0.014$, $p=0.001$ before and $0.11$ after adjustment). On
Yahoo!\,R3 the log is 2\% dense and the imputation alone is below popularity
(0.516 against 0.533), so almost every edge of a DR graph would be imputed from
a poor model; validation therefore selects IPS edges ($\lambda=0$) with
cross-fitted $W$-degrees on every split, which lie outside
Assumption~\ref{as:fixed}. DRUP (0.657) is then on par with linear propagation
over the logged graph (0.659), GF-CF (0.655) and iALS (0.663), and significantly
behind the three graph models trained with BPR: r-AdjNorm (0.674; $-0.019$,
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<10^{-3}$), LightGCN (0.671; $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.021$) and NAVIP (0.670;
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.029$). SimGCL (0.635) is below DRUP on this dataset
($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<10^{-3}$).

\paragraph{Tuning budgets} The training-free operators search many more
configurations than the trained models (Table~\ref{tab:configs}), because each
configuration costs seconds rather than a training run. Table~\ref{tab:budget}
(\ref{app:details}) gives the expected test score of DRUP when its
configuration is selected from as many random configurations, or as many
validation evaluations, as the most accurate trained model used. On Coat and
Yahoo!\,R3 the larger grid does not help DRUP: with the budget of iALS (24
configurations) it reaches 0.549 on Coat, and with that of r-AdjNorm (72) 0.657
on Yahoo!\,R3, still below both. On KuaiRec the budget matters in
configurations and not in evaluations. Counting a trained configuration with
all its epochs as one, DRUP with the six configurations of DR-JL reaches only
0.618 against 0.633; counting every validation evaluation, as each epoch of a
trained model is one, DRUP with the 103 evaluations of DR-JL reaches 0.636.
On four CPU cores, fitting the nuisances takes about 50 minutes (14 for the
cross-fitted propensity model and about 35 for the cross-fitted imputations of
the grid, shared by all DR-type operators), after which a DRUP configuration
costs about 3\,s (21 minutes for all 384), against 130--210\,s for training one
LightGCN configuration.

\paragraph{Baselines} Three observations concern the baselines. First, the loss
matters more than the architecture, and in opposite directions on the
logs with few exposures per user and on KuaiRec: pairwise training is decisive on
Yahoo!\,R3 (pointwise MF variants stay near 0.51, below popularity, whereas BPR-MF
reaches 0.646 and BPR-trained LightGCN 0.671) and on KuaiRand (BPR-MF 0.448
against 0.371 pointwise, LightGCN 0.457 against 0.406), whereas on KuaiRec the pointwise
models beat their BPR versions (LightGCN 0.627 against 0.609, MF 0.622 against
0.596). Second, iALS is the strongest trained model on Coat and competitive on
Yahoo!\,R3, but weak on KuaiRec (0.613), and the jointly learned DR models are
the strongest trained models on KuaiRec but weak on the two small logs.
No single trained baseline is strong on all datasets. Third, re-training the
split-0 selection with other seeds shows how much of a trained model's score
is seed luck. On Yahoo!\,R3 the seeds lie within 0.013 of each other (0.007 for DR-JL and
for the BPR graph models) and for the LightGCN variants on KuaiRec within 0.007; on Coat they spread by up to 0.05 for the
graph models (r-AdjNorm 0.521--0.566), and on KuaiRec by 0.009 and 0.015 for
DR-JL and MRDR, which learn their imputation jointly. The margin of DRUP over
DR-JL and MRDR on KuaiRec (0.004) is therefore smaller than their seed
variation, and differences on Coat should be read with this in mind; iALS and
the training-free operators are deterministic given the log.

Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} predicts a larger bias when propensities are small. We
therefore thinned the KuaiRec log to 10\% and 3\% of its exposures, which lowers the
median logged propensity from 0.48 to 0.045 and 0.013, re-fitted the nuisances

differences that decide the ranking, and the value of the correction lies in
the properties of the scores, not in the rankings they induce.

Table~\ref{tab:interv} tests the prediction of Theorem~\ref{thm:fair} with a known
intervention on exposure. The operators use the cross-fitted protocol, which
the theorem does not cover (Remark~\ref{rem:xfit}), so this is an empirical
check. The logged graph behaves as the theorem predicts for correlational
propagation: with frozen nuisances $\eta=+1.00$ to $+1.03$ on all four datasets, and
thinned items drop by 0.07--0.24 in mean within-user rank. On KuaiRec and Coat,
DRUP is invariant to within a few hundredths ($\eta=-0.012$ and $-0.008$) and stays
so when its nuisances are re-fitted on the thinned log, with the known or with
re-estimated propensities where they are estimated ($|\eta|\le0.022$). This is what the theorem predicts, but the controls
below show that the audit would give the same result for any estimator whose scores stay close to the
imputation, so we read it as consistent with invariance, not as evidence for it. IPS adjacency is far from invariant
($\eta=+0.14$ on KuaiRec, $+0.90$ on Coat). The uncorrected DR adjacency is as
invariant as DRUP on these data; its over-correction ($\eta=-0.19$ in
Table~\ref{tab:mc}) scales with $(1-p)/p\cdot\varepsilon^2$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}), and
the selected clips and imputations keep it small here.

Yahoo!\,R3 and KuaiRand are the exceptions, and Table~\ref{tab:tau} explains the first. On Yahoo!\,R3,
validation selects IPS edges ($\lambda=0$) with a clip of 0.1--0.2, which binds for
82\% of the logged pairs at $\tau=0.2$. A clipped IPS edge is $O_eY_e/\tau$, proportional
to the exposure itself, so DRUP inherits the elasticity of the logged graph
($\eta=+0.96$). On Coat the clip binds just as often (77\%), but with DR edges
($\lambda=1$) and an accurate imputation the clipping bias is small
(Theorem~\ref{thm:var}(c)) and invariance survives. Lowering the clip on Yahoo!\,R3
reduces the elasticity to $+0.18\pm0.11$ at $\tau=5\cdot10^{-4}$, where almost no
propensity is clipped, at a cost of 0.044 nDCG@5. KuaiRand follows the same pattern
without a clip sweep: validation selects IPS edges with the smallest clip in the grid
(0.02), which binds for 57\% of the treated logged pairs after the intervention,
and DRUP is far from invariant ($\eta=+0.64$ with frozen nuisances, $+0.65$ when
they are re-fitted; logged graph $+1.00$). The remaining elasticity and
its large spread over the thinning draws come from heavy-tailed
inverse-propensity weights on a log that is 2\% dense. Exposure invariance is
therefore a property of the estimator under its assumptions, and whether a
tuned configuration has it must be checked; the thinning audit is a simple way
to do so.

\paragraph{How informative is the audit?} With frozen nuisances the audit
updates the propensities to the known $p/2$, and an unbiased edge estimate is
then unaffected by construction; a value near zero shows that the estimator
is used as intended, not that the scores are right. Table~\ref{tab:intervextra}
adds three controls. Popularity re-fitted on the thinned log has $\eta\approx1$
and the imputation alone $\eta\approx0$ on Coat and Yahoo!\,R3 ($-0.16$ on KuaiRec), which brackets the scale. The negative
control, which keeps the propensities of the original log, should push a
debiasing estimator towards $\eta=1$. It does for IPS adjacency on all
four datasets and for every operator on Yahoo!\,R3 and KuaiRand, whose selected
configurations use IPS edges, but not for DR adjacency or DRUP on Coat ($\eta=-0.004$ and
$-0.008$) or on KuaiRec ($-0.087$ and $-0.088$). There, the scores are almost a function of the imputation (rank
correlation 0.98--0.99 with $\hat Y$), which is fitted on exposed pairs and
reacts little to a halved exposure of some items; the audit cannot detect a
misspecified propensity model in this regime, and a near-zero $\eta$ on Coat
says little beyond the invariance of $\hat Y$. DRUP-split, whose edge estimates
are less dominated by the imputation (rank correlation 0.76 on Coat, 0.58 on
Yahoo!\,R3, 0.55 on KuaiRand), is invariant on Coat and KuaiRec ($|\eta|\le0.022$ frozen
or re-fitted) and less elastic than the selected cross-fitted configuration on
Yahoo!\,R3 and KuaiRand ($\eta=+0.32$ and $+0.33$ against $+0.96$ and $+0.64$).
Both select IPS edges there, so the edge type does not explain the difference;
a fifth of the split variant's pairs carry the exposure-free imputation instead
of a clipped IPS edge, which may account for it, but we did not isolate this. The audit is thus informative for IPS-dominated configurations
and weak for configurations whose scores are close to the imputation, and it
should be reported with these controls.

three are close to each other there.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:disc}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{What the correction is for}
On the four benchmarks the walk correction does not change top-$K$
accuracy, and in simulation the uncorrected DR operator can even agree
slightly better with the full-exposure ranking, because its bias adds weight
to the imputation (Section~\ref{sec:mc}). The correction matters wherever the
\emph{value} of a propagated score is used rather than only its order within
one user's list: when scores are compared across users, as in the
exposure-capped allocation; when they are aggregated into an estimate of
full-exposure utility; and when an auditor wants to verify that expected
scores do not depend on exposure. In these uses the uncorrected estimate is
biased by 1.8 to 15 times the target over all pairs in the model of
Table~\ref{tab:mc} (0.09 to 2.2 times in the models of Table~\ref{tab:protocol}), and the
bias grows with the number of hops and with small propensities. Two limits
apply. On the unexposed candidates that are actually recommended, DRUP
estimates the full-exposure score only up to the imputation error at the
candidate (Corollary~\ref{cor:cand}), which is 12\% of the mean target at three
hops in our simulation. And the guarantees assume nuisances that do not depend
on the log: with nuisances cross-fitted on the same log, the corrected
operator is not less biased than the uncorrected one in simulation
(Section~\ref{sec:mc}), so a user of the score values needs an independent
log or the sample split of DRUP-split. We have no evidence that the correction changes
rankings, within users or under exposure caps: DRUP and DR adjacency are equivalent
within $\pm0.005$ nDCG by two one-sided tests ($p<10^{-3}$) on Yahoo!\,R3, KuaiRand and
KuaiRec and marginally on Coat ($p=0.034$; Table~S17 in the Supplementary Material). A practitioner who only needs
rankings loses nothing by using the uncorrected DR adjacency; one who uses
score values across users or pairs, audits exposure invariance, or propagates
over five hops or more, should use the corrected operator. In both cases, most of
the accuracy comes from training-free propagation over a doubly robust graph.

\paragraph{Where the evidence is weak} Five results limit what the paper can claim, and
we collect them here because each sits in a table far from the abstract.
(i) The score-level benefit needs oracle conditions. In the semi-synthetic test the
oracle target itself ranks the unexposed candidates perfectly (nDCG@20 of 1.0,
because it contains the label), whereas the estimators reach 0.37--0.38; with degree
weights taken from the imputation the corrected operator's utility error is
$-49\%$ against $-44\%$ uncorrected, and with estimated propensities it is
$+100$ to $+540\%$ for all operators. (ii) The estimator the theory covers, DRUP-split,
costs 0.062 nDCG on Yahoo!\,R3 and biases utility estimates upwards by 38--47\% because a
fifth of the pairs carry the imputation. (iii) The thinning audit has little power:
the negative control, which keeps stale propensities, does not move the scores of DR
adjacency and DRUP on Coat ($\eta=-0.008$) or KuaiRec ($\eta=-0.088$), their scores
are correlated 0.98 (Coat) with the imputation, the uncorrected DR adjacency passes
equally well, and the configurations selected on Yahoo!\,R3 and KuaiRand, the latter the
only log of platform impressions, are not invariant ($\eta=+0.96$, $+0.64$). (iv) Private
release is useful only on the largest log: it falls below popularity at every $\epsilon$ on
Coat and Yahoo!\,R3 and for $\epsilon\le4$ on KuaiRec. The certificates cover 10--26\% of
users at 20 fake profiles, and at 200 fake profiles a DR or DRUP target reaches
39\% of KuaiRec users, against 10\% under IPS. (v) The statistical evidence is thinner
than the user-level tests suggest. With the Nadeau--Bengio test over splits, DRUP is no
longer significantly ahead of DR-JL on KuaiRec ($p=0.14$ against $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.003$; MRDR
$p=0.006$), and its 0.004 margin is smaller than the seed spread of DR-JL (0.009) and MRDR (0.015). We also note
that DRUP searched 384 to 6{,}300 configurations against 6 to 108 for the trained models;
at the six configurations of DR-JL its expected KuaiRec score is 0.618 against 0.633.

\paragraph{Stakeholders}
\emph{Item providers.} Exposure invariance means that an item's expected score does not depend on
how often it was observed; it is not equal exposure, and it comes with more concentrated lists
(Table~\ref{tab:fair}) that a retrained system could entrench. Deployments should report
concentration next to invariance and use an explicit allocation mechanism. \emph{Users.} The removal
attributions of the Supplementary Material describe the model with its nuisances frozen and are
not an account of data deletion. \emph{Platforms and auditors.} DRUP is cheap and training-free
but needs correct propensities and nuisances independent of the log; the clip $\tau$ and the
control-variate weight $\lambda$ trade invariance against accuracy, and one option is to select the
most accurate configuration subject to a bound on the elasticity measured in a thinning audit,
whose limited power we described above. The audit is a first-party instrument: it needs the
log, the propensities, the deployed hyper-parameters and the ability to re-run the pipeline.
Article~40 of the Digital Services Act~\citep{dsa2022} gives vetted researchers access to data of very
large online platforms and search engines; whether it extends to re-running a proprietary
pipeline is not settled by the text, and we do not claim that it does. The Act's risk-assessment and
audit obligations (Articles~34--37, Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/436~\citep{eu2024audit}) concern
compliance of very large providers, not properties of estimators, and our results can at most
inform a risk assessment.

\paragraph{Limitations}
The analysis is static. It assumes a single logging policy and fixed potential
outcomes, whereas recommenders are retrained on the feedback they
generate~\citep{chaney2018confounding,hazrati2022evolution} and can change the
preferences they predict~\citep{perdomo2020performative}; extending the
guarantees to adaptive and slate logging~\citep{swaminathan2017slate} is open.
Proposition~\ref{prop:dep} covers dependence within a user's history, not
dependence across users. The idempotent correction relies on binary outcomes;
graded relevance would need separate unbiased estimates of the powers of $Y$.
Items that were never exposed fall outside $\pi p\ge\tau$ and are scored by the
imputation only. In our main protocol the nuisances are cross-fitted on the
same log, the Yahoo!\,R3 configuration takes degrees from edge estimates, and
the score constants are computed from the log, so the theorems do not cover
these experiments (Remark~\ref{rem:xfit}); the sample split restores them at
the price of an imputation bias on the split-off pairs and, on sparse logs,
of accuracy. The significance tests are conditional
on the single log and its fitted nuisances, and the methods searched grids of
different sizes, which we analyse but cannot fully equalise
(Section~\ref{sec:acc}). The joint-DP guarantee requires public nuisances, and on the
small Coat data the public-nuisance variant loses much of its accuracy. The
certificates are informative only against a handful of fake profiles (on
KuaiRec, 95\% of users at five profiles, 1\% at fifty), and the variance
and concentration bounds are loose by several orders of magnitude
(Section~\ref{sec:mc}). The target $F^*$ is a linear LightGCN score of the
full-exposure world, a normative choice that other stakeholders may not
share. Like every method in our comparison, DRUP selects its hyper-parameters
on a small unbiased sample; without such a sample, selection would need an
off-policy estimate of the validation metric. The propensities shipped with Coat were estimated with the help of the uniform sample that
we use for validation and testing~\citep{schnabel2016}, a possible leak that we cannot rule out.
The within-user guarantee of Proposition~\ref{prop:dep} assumes known conditional
propensities; for a fixed-size slate it is not a bound on the bias when the propensities are
estimated. The target $F^*$ contains the label of the pair it scores
(the oracle ranks the unexposed candidates perfectly), so matching it on observed
pairs is not the same as ranking well. Finally, our largest catalogue has about $10^4$
items, and the dense implementation does not scale to catalogues of $10^5$ or more.

\paragraph{Societal risks} Removing the dependence of expected scores on exposure
does not make a recommender fair to item providers: DRUP's lists are more concentrated than those
of the logged graph (Gini 0.60 against 0.34 on Coat, 1.2\% against 15\% of
the candidate items in the top-20 lists on KuaiRec), so a system retrained on its own
output could entrench a small set of items. The user-group gaps we measure are the same
under every operator. The privacy variant protects only the public item operator and
is less accurate than popularity on two of three datasets. We use only public,
anonymised data sets and no human subjects.

%======================================================================
\section{Implications}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{For research}
Propagation-level debiasing needs to consider the propagation operator itself,
not only the edges it propagates: multiplying unbiased edge estimates does not
give an unbiased multi-hop score. The repeated-walk bias is exactly
removable, and the tools that remove it (idempotent reduction and Möbius
inversion over walk-index coincidences) apply to any polynomial graph filter
built from inverse-propensity or DR estimates. Our evaluation also shows that
training-free propagation over a DR graph is a strong baseline against which
trained debiased graph recommenders should be compared, and that claims of
exposure invariance can be tested on real data by thinning exposures with a
known factor.

\paragraph{For practice}
At three hops DRUP runs at the asymptotic cost of one item Gram matrix and needs no gradient training,
so it can serve as an auditable baseline or as a candidate scorer. The
choice between the corrected and the uncorrected operator depends on whether
score values or only within-user orders matter; score values need nuisances
that do not depend on the log, such as those of DRUP-split, since with
nuisances cross-fitted on the same log they carry no unbiasedness guarantee.
On sparse logs, exact exposure
invariance requires small clips and costs accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:tau}). The
structural properties (exact attributions, certificates, a private item
operator and capped allocation) come with any linear propagation operator and
hold for the model with its nuisances held fixed; platforms should report them
together with that scope.

%======================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%======================================================================

Inverse-propensity and doubly robust graph propagation are biased beyond one
hop because walks that repeat an edge multiply its estimate with itself. We
gave the exact bias, whose worst case grows as the inverse of the propensity
clip, and removed it with an idempotent correction that is exact for any odd
number of hops and, at three hops, has the asymptotic cost of the uncorrected
operator.
With nuisances that do not depend on the log, for instance from a sample
split, the resulting estimator, DRUP, is edge-wise doubly robust for
propagation over the full-exposure graph, robust to within-user exposure
dependence to second order in the imputation error, and exposure-invariant in
expectation; in simulation, cross-fitting the nuisances on the same log does
not preserve these guarantees, so the main experiments, which cross-fit, test a
heuristic, and the sample-split variant is the one the theory covers. On a
semi-synthetic log with known exposure the correction removes the overestimate of
full-exposure utility that uncorrected operators produce. On four benchmarks with
unbiased test data the correction leaves top-$K$ accuracy and rankings unchanged,
and the most accurate method differs by dataset: iALS on Coat, BPR-trained graph
models on Yahoo!\,R3 and KuaiRand, and training-free propagation over the DR
graph on KuaiRec. Its contribution is at the level of scores. Future work includes
private nuisance models, certificates that remain informative for large
catalogues, guarantees under adaptive logging, and combining the correction with
learned propagation weights.

\section{Proofs}\label{app:proofs}

Throughout, expectations are conditional on $Y$, and ``walk'' means a
three-hop walk $u\!-\!j\!-\!v\!-\!i$ unless stated otherwise. Its edges are
$(u,j)$, $(v,j)$ and $(v,i)$.

\paragraph{Equations~\eqref{eq:drup} and~\eqref{eq:local}.}
Split the plug-in sum $(\tW\tW^\top\tW)_{ui}=\sum_{j,v}\tW_{uj}\tW_{vj}\tW_{vi}$ by walk
type (Figure~\ref{fig:walks}): (a) $v\ne u$, $j\ne i$; (b) $v=u$, $j\ne i$, giving
$\sum_{j\ne i}\tW_{uj}^2\tW_{ui}$; (c) $v\ne u$, $j=i$, giving
$\sum_{v\ne u}\tW_{ui}\tW_{vi}^2$; (d) $v=u$, $j=i$, giving $\tW_{ui}^3$. The
corrected estimator keeps (a) and replaces (b)--(d) by
$\sum_{j\ne i}C_{uj}^2W_{uj}\tW_{ui}$, $\sum_{v\ne u}\tW_{ui}C_{vi}^2W_{vi}$ and
$C_{ui}^3W_{ui}$. The difference between plug-in and corrected sums is
$\tW_{ui}\sum_{j\ne i}\Delta_{uj}+\tW_{ui}\sum_{v\ne u}\Delta_{vi}+(\tW_{ui}^3-C_{ui}^3W_{ui})
=\tW_{ui}(r_u-\Delta_{ui})+\tW_{ui}(\kappa_i-\Delta_{ui})+(\tW_{ui}^3-C_{ui}^3W_{ui})$,
which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:drup}. For Eq.~\eqref{eq:local},
$(\tilde w_uG)_i=\sum_{j\ne i}\tW_{uj}(\tW^\top\tW)_{ji}+\tW_{ui}\sum_vC_{vi}^2W_{vi}
=(\tW\tW^\top\tW)_{ui}-\tW_{ui}\kappa_i+\tW_{ui}\sum_vC_{vi}^2W_{vi}$, and substituting
into Eq.~\eqref{eq:drup} cancels the $\kappa_i$ terms. \qed

\paragraph{Theorem~\ref{thm:unbiased}.}
By the decomposition above, the corrected estimator is a sum over walks of
$\prod_{e}C_e^{k_e}W_e$, the product running over the \emph{distinct} edges of the
walk with multiplicities $k_e$. Under Assumptions~\ref{as:unconf}--\ref{as:fixed}
the $W_e$ of distinct edges are independent, so the expectation factorises,
with $\E W_e=\hat Y_e+p_e(Y_e-\hat Y_e)/\bar p_e=Y_e+\xi_e$. The target walk
monomial is $\prod_eC_e^{k_e}Y_e^{k_e}=\prod_eC_e^{k_e}Y_e$ because
$Y_e\in\{0,1\}$. The one-hop term is unbiased edge by edge. \qed

\paragraph{Corollary~\ref{cor:cand}.}
Given $O_{ui}=0$, $W_{ui}=\hat Y_{ui}$ is deterministic and the other edge
estimates keep their distribution (Assumption~\ref{as:unconf}). The edge $(u,i)$
belongs to the one-hop term and to exactly the walks of types (b), (c) and (d).
In each of them it contributes $C_{ui}^{k}\hat Y_{ui}$ instead of $C_{ui}^kY_{ui}$,
and every other distinct edge contributes its mean $Y_e$. After the idempotent
reduction, $F^*_{ui}$ is affine in $Y_{ui}$, so
$\E[s_{ui}\mid O_{ui}=0]=F^*_{ui}|_{Y_{ui}\leftarrow\hat Y_{ui}}=F^*_{ui}-(Y_{ui}-\hat Y_{ui})\,\partial F^*_{ui}/\partial Y_{ui}$.
The derivative collects $aC_{ui}$ from the one-hop term,
$bC_{ui}\sum_{j\ne i}C_{uj}^2Y_{uj}$ from type (b),
$bC_{ui}\sum_{v\ne u}C_{vi}^2Y_{vi}$ from type (c) and $bC_{ui}^3$ from type (d).
For IPS edges, $W_{ui}=0$ given $O_{ui}=0$. \qed

\paragraph{Theorem~\ref{thm:khop}.}
Fix $u$ and $i$. For an assignment $\varphi$ of values to the free variables, let
$\pi(\varphi)$ be its equality pattern. Two walk edges $(U_s,J_t)$ and
$(U_{s'},J_{t'})$ coincide if and only if $U_s,U_{s'}$ share a user block and
$J_t,J_{t'}$ share an item block, so the distinct edges and their
multiplicities $k_e$ depend on $\pi(\varphi)$ only. For a pattern $\pi$, let
$F_\pi(\varphi)=\prod_\epsilon X^{(k_\epsilon)}_{\varphi(\epsilon)}$, the product over the
distinct edges $\epsilon$ of $\pi$, with $X^{(k)}=C^{\odot k}\odot W$ (corrected) or
$X^{(k)}=\tW^{\odot k}$ (plug-in). The plug-in $K$-hop product equals
$\sum_\pi S^{\mathrm{plug}}_\pi$ and the corrected estimator is
$\sum_\pi S^{\mathrm{corr}}_\pi$, where $S_\pi=\sum_{\varphi:\pi(\varphi)=\pi}F_\pi(\varphi)$. The two
coincide for patterns without a repeated edge, which gives the form of
$\hat T^{(K)}$ once each $S_\pi$ is written through unconstrained sums. For
$\sigma\ge\pi$ let $g_\pi(\sigma)=\sum_{\varphi:\pi(\varphi)\ge\sigma}F_\pi(\varphi)$: variables in the
same block of $\sigma$ are equal and values of different blocks are unrestricted,
so $g_\pi(\sigma)$ is a single tensor contraction over the blocks of $\sigma$ (with
the blocks of $U_0$ and $J_{r+1}$ fixed to $u$ and $i$). Grouping the terms of
$g_\pi(\sigma)$ by their exact pattern gives $g_\pi(\sigma)=\sum_{\tau\ge\sigma}f_\pi(\tau)$ with
$f_\pi(\tau)=\sum_{\varphi:\pi(\varphi)=\tau}F_\pi(\varphi)$. Möbius inversion on the interval
$[\pi,\hat1]$ of the product of the two partition lattices~\citep{rota1964} yields
$S_\pi=f_\pi(\pi)=\sum_{\sigma\ge\pi}\mu(\pi,\sigma)\,g_\pi(\sigma)$, where
$\mu(\pi,\sigma)=\prod_{B}(-1)^{b_B-1}(b_B-1)!$ over the blocks $B$ of $\sigma$ in both
lattices and $b_B$ is the number of blocks of $\pi$ merged into $B$. Finally, for
every $\varphi$ with $\pi(\varphi)=\pi$ the distinct edges are independent, so
$\E F^{\mathrm{corr}}_\pi(\varphi)=\prod_\epsilon C_{\varphi(\epsilon)}^{k_\epsilon}Y_{\varphi(\epsilon)}$, which is the
corresponding monomial of $(\tY\tY^\top)^r\tY$ since $Y$ is binary. The number of
contraction terms is the number of pairs $(\pi,\sigma)$ with $\sigma\ge\pi$ and $\pi$
containing a repeated edge, which is 5, 99 and 2{,}790 for $K=3,5,7$. \qed

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:dep}.}
With correct propensities $\E W_e=Y_e$, and for distinct $e,f$ in the same row
$\mathrm{Cov}(W_e,W_f)=(Y_e-\hat Y_e)(Y_f-\hat Y_f)\,\mathrm{Cov}(O_e,O_f)/(p_ep_f)$, so
$|\mathrm{Cov}(W_e,W_f)|\le\rho\,\varepsilon_e\varepsilon_f$. In a type (a) walk the edge $(u,j)$
lies in row $u$ and the edges $(v,j),(v,i)$ in row $v\ne u$; rows are independent,
so $\E[W_{uj}W_{vj}W_{vi}]=Y_{uj}(Y_{vj}Y_{vi}+\mathrm{Cov}(W_{vj},W_{vi}))$ and the bias of
the walk is at most $C_{uj}C_{vj}C_{vi}\rho\,\varepsilon_{vj}\varepsilon_{vi}$. A corrected type (b)
walk contributes $C_{uj}^2C_{ui}W_{uj}W_{ui}$ with two edges in row $u$, with bias
at most $C_{uj}^2C_{ui}\rho\,\varepsilon_{uj}\varepsilon_{ui}$. Type (c) walks have their two
distinct edges in different rows and type (d) walks a single edge, so they are
unbiased. Summing gives the bound. For $k$ of $n$ items drawn uniformly,
$\Pr(O_e=O_f=1)=k(k-1)/(n(n-1))$, so
$|\mathrm{Cov}(O_e,O_f)|/(p_ep_f)=(n-k)/(k(n-1))\le1/k$. \qed

\paragraph{Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}.}
By the decomposition of Eq.~\eqref{eq:drup}, the plug-in minus the corrected
operator is $\tW_{ui}\sum_{j\ne i}\Delta_{uj}+\tW_{ui}\sum_{v\ne u}\Delta_{vi}+(\tW_{ui}^3-C_{ui}^3W_{ui})$.
Given $O_{ui}=0$, $\tW_{ui}=C_{ui}\hat Y_{ui}$ is deterministic and the other
$\Delta_e$ keep their distribution. With correct propensities and binary $Y$,
$\E\Delta_e=C_e^2(\E W_e^2-\E W_e)=C_e^2(\Var W_e+Y_e^2-Y_e)=C_e^2V_e$ with
$V_e=\frac{1-p_e}{p_e}\varepsilon_e^2$, which gives the stated bias, the last term being
$C_{ui}^3(\hat Y_{ui}^3-\hat Y_{ui})$. Part (a) uses $(1-p)/p\ge1/\max_vp_{vi}-1$. For
(b), with $\mu_e=\E W_e\in[0,1]$, $\E W_e^2-\E W_e=\Var W_e-\mu_e(1-\mu_e)\le\Var W_e
=p_e(1-p_e)\varepsilon_e^2/\bar p_e^2$. If $p_e\ge\tau$ this is
$\frac{1-p_e}{p_e}\varepsilon_e^2\le(1/\tau-1)\varepsilon_e^2$; if $p_e<\tau\le1/2$ it is
$p_e(1-p_e)\varepsilon_e^2/\tau^2<\tau(1-\tau)\varepsilon_e^2/\tau^2$ because $p(1-p)$ increases on
$[0,1/2]$. At $p_e=\tau$, $\mu_e=Y_e$ and the bound is attained; setting all
$p_{vi}=\tau$ gives a bias $C_{ui}\hat Y_{ui}(1/\tau-1)\sum_{v\ne u}C_{vi}^2\varepsilon_{vi}^2$, so the
worst case is $\Theta(1/\tau)$. For (c), IPS gives $\tW_{ui}=0$ and $C_{ui}^3W_{ui}=0$
given $O_{ui}=0$. \qed

\paragraph{Theorem~\ref{thm:var}.}
(a) $\Var(W_e)=p_e(1-p_e)\varepsilon_e^2/\bar p_e^2\le\varepsilon_e^2/(4\tau^2)$ for any $\hat p$,
since $p(1-p)\le1/4$ and $\bar p_e\ge\tau$. If $\hat p=p$, then $\bar p=\max(p,\tau)$: if
$p_e\ge\tau$ the variance is at most $(1/\tau-1)\varepsilon_e^2$, otherwise at most
$p_e\varepsilon_e^2/\tau^2<\varepsilon_e^2/\tau$. Without $\hat p=p$ the bound $\varepsilon_e^2/\tau$ can
fail (e.g., $p=0.5$, $\hat p=0.01$, $\tau=0.05$ gives $100\,\varepsilon_e^2>20\,\varepsilon_e^2$).
(b) $\hat T_{ui}$ is multilinear in the independent $W_e$ and hence affine in each
of them. The Efron--Stein inequality~\citep{efron1981jackknife} gives
$\Var(\hat T)\le\sum_e\E[(\partial_e\hat T)^2]\Var(W_e)$. The derivative $\partial_e\hat T$
is a sum over the walks through $e$ of $C$-weights times at most two other
independent $W_f$, and with $\hat p=p$, $\mu_f=Y_f\in[0,1]$ and
$\E W_f^2=\mu_f^2+\Var W_f\le1+1/\tau$ by (a). Minkowski's
inequality bounds $\|\partial_e\hat T\|_2\le(1+1/\tau)\Lambda_e$.
(c) By Theorem~\ref{thm:unbiased}, the bias is at most
$\sum_{\text{walks}}|\kappa_{\text{walk}}|\,[\prod_f(Y_f+|\xi_f|)-\prod_fY_f]\le((1+\bar\xi)^3-1)\sum_{j,v}C_{uj}C_{vj}C_{vi}$,
and $(1+x)^3-1\le3x(1+x)^2$. With $\hat p=p$, $|\xi_e|=(1-p_e/\tau)_+\varepsilon_e$.
(d) Changing $O_e$ moves $W_e$ by at most $\varepsilon_e/\tau$. Every edge estimate
satisfies $|W_f|\le B$, so $|\partial_e\hat T|\le B^2\Lambda_e$, and McDiarmid's
inequality~\citep{mcdiarmid1989} applies. \qed

\paragraph{Theorem~\ref{thm:fair}.}
By Definition~\ref{def:elast} the estimator uses the post-intervention
propensities, and if $\pi p\ge\tau$ the clip does not bind, so they remain
correct. DRUP: by Theorem~\ref{thm:unbiased}, $\E[s]=F^*$,
and by Corollary~\ref{cor:cand}, $\E[s\mid O_{ui}=0]$ is $F^*$ with $Y_{ui}$ replaced by
the fixed $\hat Y_{ui}$; neither depends on $\pi$. Logged graph with fixed $C$: $\E[O_eY_e]=p_eY_e$,
and every walk that ends at $i$ contains $k\ge1$ edges incident to $i$, so its
expectation scales as $\pi^k$ and $\eta_i$ is an average of such $k$. Uncorrected
DR: by Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}, $\E[s_{ui}\mid O_{ui}=0]$ equals the DRUP target plus
$C_{ui}\hat Y_{ui}(R_u^{(i)}+K_i(\pi))$ minus a term free of $\pi$; $R_u^{(i)}$ involves
no edge of item $i$, and $K_i(\pi)=\sum_{v\ne u}C_{vi}^2\frac{1-\pi p_{vi}}{\pi p_{vi}}\varepsilon_{vi}^2$ is
strictly decreasing in $\pi$. \qed

\section{Experimental details}\label{app:details}

\paragraph{Training-free operators}
All operators share the score $s=s_1/c_1+\beta s_3/c_3$ (Section~\ref{sec:drup}) with
$\beta\in\{0,0.03,0.1,0.3,1,3,10\}$ or the three-hop term alone; the five-hop
variants add $\gamma s_5/c_5$ with $\gamma\in\{0.03,0.1,0.3,1,3\}$. The grids are:
normalisation exponent $\alpha\in\{0.3,0.5,0.7\}$ (Coat), $\{0.5,0.7\}$
(Yahoo!\,R3, KuaiRand) and $\{0.3,0.5\}$ (KuaiRec); clip
$\tau\in\{0.01,0.02,0.05,0.1,0.2\}$ (Coat), $\{0.1,0.2\}$ (Yahoo!\,R3),
$\{0.02,0.05,0.1\}$ (KuaiRand) and $\{0.05,0.1,0.2\}$ (KuaiRec); imputation ridge
$\lambda\in\{1,5,20\}$ (Coat), $\{20\}$ (Yahoo!\,R3, KuaiRand) and $\{5,20\}$
(KuaiRec); additive or low-rank (rank 32) imputation; control-variate weight
$\in\{0,0.25,0.5,1\}$ on Yahoo!\,R3, $\{0,0.5,1\}$ on KuaiRand and $1$ elsewhere; and degree source $\in\{\hat Y,\ \text{cross-fitted }W\}$ for
the debiased operators. The degree source is a searched hyper-parameter.
$\hat Y$-degrees satisfy Assumption~\ref{as:fixed} whenever $\hat Y$ does;
cross-fitted $W$-degrees do not, since the weight at a pair then depends on
the exposures of other pairs, so configurations with $W$-degrees (selected on
Yahoo!\,R3) lie outside the theorems. Only $\hat Y$-degrees make the
certificates of Theorem~S3 informative. EASE uses
$\lambda\in\{10,100,1000\}$; GF-CF uses four settings of (ideal low-pass weight, singular vectors):
$(0,0)$, $(0.3,64)$, $(0.3,256)$ and $(1,256)$. BSPM~\citep{choi2023bspm} uses blurring time
$\in\{1,2\}$, sharpening time $\in\{0,0.1,0.3\}$ and ideal low-pass weight
$\in\{0,0.3\}$ with one Euler step each. On the DR graph, EASE, GF-CF and BSPM
search the same nuisance grid as DR adjacency. The sample-split variants are
run for five draws of the split-off pairs; each draw is tuned separately, and
the tables report the mean over draws. The propensity model is
the one shipped with Coat and a logistic exposure model on user and item
exposure rates elsewhere; on Yahoo!\,R3 it was selected on validation data
against an outcome-dependent Naive-Bayes model. All nuisances are cross-fitted
over ten random folds of pairs, except for the sample-split variants, whose
imputation (and propensity model, where it is estimated) is fitted on a random
20\% of the pairs.

\paragraph{Trained models}
The grid combines the learning rates $10^{-2}$, $3\cdot10^{-3}$ and $10^{-3}$ with the
weight decays $10^{-5}$, $10^{-4}$ and (Coat, and the MF models on Yahoo!\,R3)
$10^{-3}$. Embeddings have 64 dimensions (Coat: 32 or 64). The graph models with
the widest search (LightGCN and r-AdjNorm on Yahoo!\,R3, the pointwise
and BPR LightGCN on KuaiRec, all graph models on Coat) also search one to three
layers and 64 or 128 dimensions (Coat: 32 or 64); the others use two layers.
PDA searches $\gamma\in\{0.1,0.2\}$, r-AdjNorm $r\in\{0.3,0.7\}$, SimGCL the
contrastive weight $\in\{0.1,0.5\}$ (noise $0.1$, temperature $0.2$, InfoNCE over at
most 2{,}048 users and items of a batch) and MACR $c\in\{0.1,0.3\}$; iALS searches
the confidence $\alpha\in\{3,10,30\}$ and the ridge $\in\{1,10,100,1000\}$. DR-JL and MRDR
learn an MF imputation model jointly with the prediction
model~\citep{wang2019drjl,guo2021mrdr}. BPR models draw one uniformly sampled
negative per positive. Batch sizes are 256 (Coat), 1{,}024 (Yahoo!\,R3), 8{,}192
(KuaiRand) and 32{,}768 (KuaiRec, with 2M sampled pairs per epoch for the
pointwise losses); training runs for at most 100, 60, 40 and 30 epochs. Because
the training log is the same for every split, each configuration is trained
once and evaluated after every epoch on the validation part of every split.
Each split stops on its own validation score (patience 5 on Coat and
Yahoo!\,R3, 3 otherwise) and selects its own configuration and epoch; training
continues until every split has stopped, and a split never uses epochs after
its own stopping point, so no split's selection depends on another split's
validation users, who may be its test users. The split-0 selection is
re-trained with three (KuaiRec, KuaiRand: two) further seeds to measure seed
variance. We did not run DICE, StableDR and methods that train on the uniform
data (CausE, AutoDebias): in our protocol the uniform data serve only for
selection and testing, for every method. Table~\ref{tab:configs} lists the
number of configurations each method was selected from.

\paragraph{Evaluation and tests}
Coat splits each user's candidates (30\% validation), Yahoo!\,R3, KuaiRand and
KuaiRec split the test users (30\% validation), over 10, 5, 5 and 5 random splits.
Users without a positive candidate are skipped. For a pair of methods, each
test user's metric difference is averaged over the splits in which the user
is in the test part, and we report the mean difference over users with a
95\% $t$-interval and the paired $t$-test. The Holm family is every comparison
run for a dataset, with all references together (DRUP, DR adjacency, five-hop
DRUP and DRUP-split against every other method); the Wilcoxon signed-rank
$p$-values are adjusted over the same family. Within-reference adjustments are
in the result files. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test gives the same conclusions except for the
five-hop DRUP against DR comparison on KuaiRec ($p=0.66$ against 0.011) and for
DRUP-split against pointwise LightGCN on KuaiRec ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.48$ against $3\cdot10^{-4}$). The
corrected resampled $t$-test over split means~\citep{nadeau2003} is far more
conservative with five or ten splits and is reported in the result files.
All tests condition on the training log and the fitted nuisances.

\paragraph{Hardware and code}
All experiments ran on four CPU cores with 16\,GB of memory, except the KuaiRand
debiased and sample-split operators, which ran on a Google Colab T4 GPU (same code and
seeds; per-user ranking metrics are computed once per score matrix and match the
per-user routine to $10^{-15}$). GF-CF uses a randomised SVD, so its scores vary by
about 0.002 between runs.

\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================

Graph-based recommender systems represent users and items as nodes and
logged interactions as edges, and they score a user--item pair by aggregating
signals along multi-hop walks~\cite{he2020lightgcn,shen2021gfcf}. The logged
graph, however, is not the preference graph. An edge exists only if the
platform \emph{exposed} the item and the user then responded, so the graph
records the exposure policy as much as the user's taste. A recent survey of
causal learning in graph-based recommendation~\cite{yu2026survey} traces
several open problems of the field, among them fairness, interpretability and
out-of-distribution behaviour, to message passing over this observational
graph, and it lists theoretical guarantees and privacy as open directions.

Two families of corrections exist. \emph{Loss-level} debiasing re-weights the
training objective by inverse propensities or doubly robust (DR)
terms~\cite{schnabel2016,wang2019drjl,saito2020unbiased}, but it leaves the
propagation operator of a graph model untouched. \emph{Propagation-level}
debiasing changes the propagated graph itself, for example by weighting each
edge by its inverse propensity~\cite{kim2022navip} or by learning
popularity-aware aggregation weights~\cite{zhou2023apda,dpaa2026}. This paper
starts from a simple observation about the second family. An inverse-propensity
edge estimate $W_e=O_eY_e/p_e$ is unbiased for the potential outcome $Y_e$,
but a walk that traverses $e$ twice contributes $W_e^2$, and
$\E[W_e^2]=Y_e/p_e\neq Y_e$. The same happens for DR edge estimates. Walks that
repeat an edge are part of every multi-hop operator: the back-and-forth step
$u\to j\to u$ of a three-hop walk is one. Their excess is largest where the
propensity is smallest, that is, on the rarely exposed edges that debiasing is
meant to recover.

We make this observation precise and remove the resulting bias exactly. Four
points qualify its practical reach, and we state them up front. First, the
guarantees need nuisances (propensities, imputation, degree weights and score
constants) that do not depend on the log. Cross-fitting over pairs, the
standard remedy, does not provide them for products of several edge
estimates, and in simulation the correction then no longer reduces bias; a
sample split does, and we treat this variant, DRUP-split, as the estimator
that the theory covers. Second, for ranking the \emph{unexposed} candidates of
a user with inverse-propensity edges, the repeated walks through the candidate
vanish at three hops, and the bias only appears from five hops on; for DR
edges it is present at three hops. Third, on four public benchmarks removing
the bias does not change top-$K$ accuracy or rankings, also under exposure
caps. Fourth, the correction does matter where score values are used: on a
semi-synthetic log built from KuaiRec's fully observed matrix, uncorrected
propagation overestimates the full-exposure utility of an allocation by
18--29\%, and the corrected operator is within 2\%, provided that the
propensities are correct and the propagation weights are fixed a priori. The
contribution is therefore at the level of scores, not accuracy.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Diagnosis.} We give the exact conditional bias of inverse-propensity
and DR graph propagation on unexposed candidates. It is unbounded as
propensities vanish, and under a propensity clip $\tau$ its worst case is
$\Theta(1/\tau)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}).
\item \emph{Estimator.} DRUP (Doubly Robust, walk-Unbiased Propagation) uses
$Y_e^2=Y_e$ for binary outcomes and replaces every repeated edge by its
idempotent unbiased counterpart. The three-hop operator has the asymptotic
cost of the uncorrected one (one item Gram matrix plus $O(mn)$ reductions) and
factorises into a public item operator and per-user corrections. Möbius
inversion over the coincidence patterns of walk indices makes the correction
exact for any odd number of hops (Theorem~\ref{thm:khop}). A sample split
supplies nuisances that do not depend on the edge estimates.
\item \emph{Guarantees.} With nuisances that do not depend on the log, DRUP is
edge-wise doubly robust for propagation over the full-exposure graph
(Theorem~\ref{thm:unbiased}); on unexposed candidates it is unbiased up to the
imputation error at the candidate itself (Corollary~\ref{cor:cand}); its bias
under exposure dependence within a user's history is of second order in the
imputation error (Proposition~\ref{prop:dep}); we bound its variance and
clipping bias (Theorem~\ref{thm:var}); and its expected scores do not respond
to interventions on an item's exposure (Theorem~\ref{thm:fair}). Properties
that follow from linearity alone (attributions, certificates, joint
differential privacy, capped allocation) hold for any linear operator and are
in the Supplementary Material.
\item \emph{Evaluation.} We check the guarantees and their scope by Monte
Carlo, including the gap between the theory and cross-fitted nuisances; test
score values and score-based decisions on a semi-synthetic KuaiRec log with
known propensities; and compare DRUP with up to 26 tuned baselines on Coat~\cite{schnabel2016},
Yahoo!\,R3~\cite{marlin2009collaborative}, KuaiRand~\cite{gao2022kuairand} and
KuaiRec~\cite{gao2022kuairec}, whose test data are random or fully observed,
with per-split validation for every method, an analysis of tuning budgets,
and user-level tests with Holm correction. Real-data thinning interventions,
with a negative control, test exposure invariance.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
%======================================================================

We review the lines of work that DRUP touches. Table~\ref{tab:related}
summarises how DRUP relates to representative methods.

\paragraph{Graph-based recommendation.}
Graph neural recommenders learn user and item representations by propagating
embeddings over the user--item graph. NGCF~\cite{wang2019ngcf} stacks
non-linear propagation layers, and LightGCN~\cite{he2020lightgcn} shows that
removing feature transformations and non-linearities improves accuracy, which
reduces propagation to a weighted sum of powers of a normalised adjacency.
UltraGCN~\cite{mao2021ultragcn} approximates infinite-layer propagation with a
constraint loss, and SGL~\cite{wu2021sgl} and SimGCL~\cite{yu2022simgcl} add
contrastive views. Closed-form models are strong competitors of trained
propagation: EASE~\cite{steck2019ease} learns an item--item matrix with a
zero diagonal in closed form, and EDLAE~\cite{steck2020edlae} generalises the
constraint by penalising the diagonal, which, like our diagonal correction of
$G$, removes an item's own contribution to its score; GF-CF~\cite{shen2021gfcf} and
BSPM~\cite{choi2023bspm} interpret collaborative filtering as graph signal
filtering, and graph convolutions can trade accuracy for
diversity~\cite{isufi2021accuracy}. Reproducibility studies show that carefully
tuned simple baselines often match graph models~\cite{anelli2023myth}. All these
models propagate over the \emph{logged} graph. DRUP keeps the linear
propagation form of LightGCN and of closed-form filters but propagates over an
unbiased estimate of the full-exposure graph, and we compare it with EASE and
GF-CF on both graphs.

\paragraph{Bias and debiasing in recommendation.}
Recommendation logs are missing not at random: users see what the system
exposed and rate what they chose to rate~\cite{marlin2009collaborative}.
Surveys organise the resulting biases and their remedies~\cite{chen2023bias}
and cast debiasing as causal inference~\cite{gao2024causalsurvey}.
Propensity-based methods re-weight the loss by inverse exposure
probabilities~\cite{schnabel2016}, possibly self-normalised to reduce
variance~\cite{swaminathan2015snips}; \citet{saito2020unbiased} extend them
to implicit feedback, and \citet{liang2016exposure} model exposure as a
latent variable. Doubly robust learning combines inverse propensities with
an imputation model~\cite{wang2019drjl}, and later variants reduce its bias
and variance~\cite{guo2021mrdr,dai2022gdr,li2023stabledr} or learn
information-theoretic representations~\cite{wang2020cvib}. A small uniformly
logged sample can supervise the debiasing itself~\cite{chen2021autodebias}.
Causal approaches to popularity bias remove its direct effect by
counterfactual inference~\cite{wei2021macr}, adjust for it as a
confounder~\cite{zhang2021pda}, or disentangle interest from
conformity~\cite{zheng2021dice}. In this journal, popularity debiasing has
been studied from the user and item side jointly~\cite{boratto2021connecting},
with a controllable accuracy trade-off~\cite{okamura2024flexibly}, through
invariant learning with biased imputation~\cite{bai2025invariant}, and by
causal deconfounding of multi-criteria ratings~\cite{guo2026confounding}.
These methods correct the loss or the output of a model. DRUP applies IPS and
DR estimation to the \emph{edges} of the propagated graph, and its
control-variate family interpolates between the two.

\paragraph{Debiasing inside graph propagation.}
Closest to our work are methods that change neighbour aggregation.
NAVIP~\cite{kim2022navip} weights each edge by its inverse propensity inside
LightGCN. r-AdjNorm~\cite{zhao2022radjnorm} shows that the normalisation
exponent of the adjacency controls how strongly popularity is amplified, and
tunes it; graph convolutions provably amplify popularity
bias~\cite{chen2024gcnpop}, and deconfounding can reduce the amplification of
user-level biases~\cite{wang2021decrs}. APDA~\cite{zhou2023apda} learns an adaptive inverse-popularity
weight per edge, CAGED~\cite{que2025caged} derives aggregation weights from a
backdoor adjustment, and DPAA~\cite{dpaa2026} learns interaction- and
layer-wise weights to counter the amplification of popularity by repeated
message passing. These methods change \emph{which} adjacency is propagated and
target popularity-related metrics. None of them analyses what happens to an
unbiased edge estimate when the re-weighted adjacency is multiplied with
itself over several hops. Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} shows that the product is
then biased, by a term of order $1/p$ that concentrates on rarely exposed
items, and DRUP removes this term exactly for any odd number of hops. Our linear
propagation baseline over the logged graph searches the normalisation exponent
and is therefore a training-free r-AdjNorm; we also train r-AdjNorm and NAVIP.

\paragraph{Unbiased estimation of polynomials under missingness.}
The walk correction belongs to a classical idea: an unbiased estimator of a
polynomial in unknown quantities must avoid products of the same noisy
measurement, as in U-statistics~\cite{hoeffding1948}. For covariance matrices
estimated from Bernoulli-masked data, the off-diagonal entries of the masked
Gram matrix need a $1/p^2$ correction and the diagonal a $1/p$
correction~\cite{lounici2014}; our diagonal-corrected item operator $G$ is the
analogue for inverse-propensity and DR edges with heterogeneous propensities.
Unbiased subgraph counts from sampled edges~\cite{tsourakakis2009doulion},
and the passage from homomorphism counts to injective counts by Möbius
inversion on the partition lattice~\cite{rota1964}, are the combinatorial
counterparts of our $K$-hop construction. Removing walks that revisit vertices
or edges is also standard in network analysis: non-backtracking
operators~\cite{krzakala2013} and self-avoiding-walk
statistics~\cite{hopkins2017bayesian} discard backtracking walks to control
the spectrum or the variance. Our correction differs in that it keeps the
repeated walks and replaces each repeated factor by its unbiased
counterpart, so that the estimator targets the full walk sum, repeats
included. In recommendation, \citet{schnabel2020item} debias item-to-item
co-occurrence statistics with propensities and a small annotated sample. The
reduction itself is therefore not new. What is new is its combination with
inverse-propensity and DR edge estimates and degree weights in graph
recommendation, the exact bias of the uncorrected operators, the
public-operator form, and the consequences for exposure invariance. Our
treatment of nuisances follows double machine learning, whose cross-fitting we
adopt~\cite{chernozhukov2018dml}, and our clipping and control-variate choices
mirror doubly robust off-policy evaluation~\cite{dudik2011doubly}.

\paragraph{Fairness in recommendation.}
Fairness notions in recommendation cover users and items, groups and
individuals, and outcomes and exposure~\cite{wang2023fairness}; a unifying
account of their measurement is given by~\cite{amigo2023fairness}, and
provider fairness and its relation to data characteristics have been analysed
in~\cite{gomez2022provider,deldjoo2021explaining}. On the item side, exposure can be allocated
in proportion to merit~\cite{singh2018exposure}, amortised over
time~\cite{biega2018equity} or under uncertainty about
merit~\cite{singh2021uncertainty}, and two-sided allocations can be guaranteed
by constrained matching~\cite{patro2020fairrec}; expected exposure evaluates
stochastic rankings against merit~\cite{diaz2020expected}, and
multi-stakeholder views weigh users, providers and the
platform~\cite{abdollahpouri2020multistakeholder,ekstrand2022fairness}.
Fairness can also be audited by counterfactual
reasoning~\cite{cornacchia2023auditing}. DRUP contributes a
causal item-side property of the merit \emph{estimate}, exposure invariance:
its expected scores do not respond to interventions on an item's exposure
(Theorem~\ref{thm:fair}). It is not a fairness criterion. Merit-based
allocation can be applied to any merit estimate, and what it requires is an
estimate whose errors do not depend on past exposure; exposure invariance is
one such property, relative to the full-exposure target $F^*$ as the notion of
merit. It can increase the concentration of recommendations, and the
distributive mechanism is an explicit allocation such as the exposure-capped
$b$-matching of Theorem~\ref{thm:alloc}, related to~\cite{patro2020fairrec}.

\paragraph{Explanations, robustness and privacy.}
Explainable recommendation is surveyed by~\cite{zhang2020explainable}. For graph models,
GNNExplainer~\cite{ying2019gnnexplainer} and CF-GNNExplainer~\cite{lucic2022cfgnn}
search for important or minimal sets of edges, PRINCE~\cite{ghazimatin2020prince}
finds minimal sets of a user's actions whose removal changes a random-walk
recommendation, and CountER~\cite{tan2021counter} optimises counterfactual
explanations of aspect-based models. Counterfactual explanations have a legal
motivation~\cite{wachter2018counterfactual} and hidden
assumptions~\cite{barocas2020hidden}, and Shapley attributions depend on their
baseline~\cite{kumar2020shapley}. Recommenders can be manipulated by injected
profiles~\cite{lam2004shilling,li2016poisoning,fang2018poisoning}, and shilling
groups can be detected with graph convolutions~\cite{wang2022shilling};
PORE~\cite{jia2023pore} certifies robustness by ensembling models trained on
sub-sampled data. Private recommenders perturb item--item
statistics~\cite{mcsherry2009netflix}, factorisation models~\cite{liu2024dpmf} or
matrix completion under joint differential privacy~\cite{jain2018dpmc}, and
federated approaches keep ratings on the device~\cite{chai2021fedmf}. Any
linear propagation operator with fixed nuisances, DRUP included, admits exact
removal attributions, closed-form certificates and a jointly private release
of its item operator with an analytically calibrated Gaussian
mechanism~\cite{balle2018analytic,hsu2014matchings}; we state and test these
properties in the Supplementary Material.
